# Being, Relation, and Gift

### A Theological Exposition of the Core

---

## Preface

The checker in this repository rests on a short formal statement, set out
axiom by axiom in [ttc-core(5)](core-edition.md). A formal statement has to
be terse, and to be computable it has to give everything a name. The names
it chose served their purpose, but they are not the language of theology,
and a reader trained in the discipline will rightly ask what the axioms
*mean* before asking what they compute.

This essay answers that question. It sets out the theology of the core in
the ordinary vocabulary of the schools and the magisterium: participation,
relation, person, procession, consent, bond, communion, analogy. It
proceeds as an exposition, not as a disputation. It states a position,
gives the reasons for it, notes the principal difficulties, and indicates
where the position is taught, where it is only fitting, and where it is
our own construction. A short concordance at the end connects each
traditional term to the notation of the formal core.

Two disclaimers belong at the outset. First, nothing here is offered as
demonstration. The core is an argument from fittingness, in the sense
St Thomas gives the term when he distinguishes a reason that proves its
conclusion from one that "shows the congruity of the consequent effects".[^1]
Its claim is that a single structure of giving and receiving runs through
the doctrine of God, the doctrine of creation and the law of marriage, and
that recognizing it clarifies each. Second, everything here is submitted to
the judgment of the Church. Where the core and the magisterium appear to
diverge, the presumption is that the core has been stated badly. The essay
records one place where that presumption has already proved correct.

---

## Part One: Being as Gift

### 1. The one and the many, once more

The oldest problem in metaphysics is how the many can be really many and
yet belong to one order. The Christian answer, worked out by the Fathers and
given its classical form by the medieval masters, is not a compromise
between unity and multiplicity but a doctrine of *participation*. There is
one source of being. Everything else has being from that source, really
and as its own, but always as received.[^2] The many are many because they
receive in different measures and in different ways. They are one order
because they all receive from the one source.

Christian revelation adds what philosophy could not supply. The one source
is not solitary. In God himself there is giving and receiving: the Father
gives the whole divine nature to the Son, and the Father and the Son
together breathe forth the Spirit. Here plurality arises without any
inequality, any loss, or any separation of nature. If that is so, then giving and
receiving are not marks of imperfection to be left behind as one ascends
toward the first principle. They belong to the highest perfection there
is.

The core is an attempt to take this seriously and follow it consistently.
It asks what follows if giving and receiving are treated as the basic
relations of reality, in God and in creatures alike, and if the strict
discipline of analogy is kept at every step. Its conclusions about
marriage, about the person and about the law of the Church are meant to
follow from that question, not to be added to it.

### 2. Participation: being received

The first axiom of the core says that every creature receives its being
from God alone, and that every creature acts. Both halves are traditional.

That being is received is the doctrine of creation itself. The creature
does not have its existence of itself. It has it from another, as an
*esse participatum*, and the relation of dependence is not an accident
added to a creature already constituted. It is what it is to be a
creature.[^3] Two corollaries matter for what follows. First, this
relation is real in the creature and only a relation of reason in God.[^4]
Creation changes nothing in God, and God does not become one term in a pair
alongside the world. Second, there is only one giver of being. No creature
gives being in the strict sense to another, however much it may cause
change, generation or growth. Every created cause works on what is already
given.

That every creature acts is the old axiom *agere sequitur esse*: activity
follows on being, and a being that could not act would not truly have
being of its own.[^5] Fr Norris Clarke made this the centre of his
metaphysics. To be, for a finite being, is to be both *in itself* and
*toward others*, and the self-communication that is perfect in God is
imitated, at every level, in the active self-sharing of creatures.[^6] The
core accepts this and draws a consequence that will matter later. Since
every creature both receives and acts, "receptive" and "active" cannot
serve as names for two *kinds* of creature. Every creature is both.

### 3. The person as substance-in-relation

Classical metaphysics gave relation the weakest share of being among the
categories: an accident, and the most tenuous one.[^7] Trinitarian
theology overturned this at the summit. In God the persons are *subsisting
relations*: the Father is nothing other than paternity subsisting, the Son
filiation subsisting.[^8] Joseph Ratzinger called this the moment when
relation appeared, for the first time, as a primordial mode of being on a
level with substance.[^9]

Clarke drew from this a thesis about every person, created or uncreated:
to be a person is to be *substance-in-relation*. A person stands in
itself, and is by nature turned toward others. These are not two facts
about it but two dimensions of one act of existing.[^10] He also argued,
more controversially, that *receptivity* is not as such an imperfection.
The Son receives everything from the Father and lacks nothing, so to
receive, in the right mode, can be a perfection.[^11] David Schindler
pressed the point further, asking whether receptivity is not co-original
with activity in every finite being, and the exchange between them in
*Communio* remains the best short introduction to the question.[^12]

The core takes the following position on this question. Receiving and giving
are not *properties* of a person, as height or temperament are. They are
*positions in a relation*. One is a receiver *of something, from someone*,
and a giver *of something, to someone*. Stated so, a familiar argument
turns out to say nothing. Some writers have proposed that man is the giver
and woman the receiver, as God gives and the creature receives. But every
human person, man or woman, receives being from God, and every human
person, man or woman, gives and acts. Taken as properties, "receptive" and
"giving" hold of everyone and so distinguish no one.[^13] The Church's own
teaching agrees: God is neither man nor woman, and the analogy of the
Bridegroom and the Bride, where Scripture uses it, is an analogy, not a
template for the sexes.[^14]

## Part Two: Origin

### 4. Relations of origin in God

In God, the persons are distinguished by relations of origin and by nothing
else: "in God all things are one, where there is no opposition of
relation".[^15] The Father is from no one, *principium sine principio*.[^16]
The Son is from the Father alone, by generation. The Spirit proceeds from
the Father and the Son "as from one principle and by one spiration".[^17]

Two features of this doctrine are central to the core.

The first is the *unity of principle*. The Spirit does not proceed from two
sources, as if the Father contributed one part of the procession and the Son
another. Father and Son are one principle of the Spirit, because the power
by which they breathe him forth is one and the same power, the divine
nature, which the Son has received whole from the Father.[^18] Here the
unity of principle is not a rule that the persons happen to keep. It could
not be otherwise. Two principles would require an opposition of relation
between Father and Son with respect to the Spirit, and there is none.

The second is that, in God, *to receive and to give are found in one and the
same person*. The Son is from the Father. With the Father, he is principle
of the Spirit. And what he gives, he has received: the Council of Florence
teaches that the Son has from the Father that the Spirit proceeds from
him.[^19] The Son is therefore the decisive case against any opposition
between receptivity and fecundity. He is wholly received, and he is fully
fruitful, and he is equal to the Father in all things.[^20] Origin implies
order, but not rank. That is the content of the *homoousios*.[^21]

### 5. Generation among creatures

The core holds that something analogous to the unity of principle obtains
in human generation, and it is here that the analogy has to be stated with
the greatest care.

A child has two parents. In what sense can they be said to be *one*
principle of the child? Scripture supplies the ground. The spouses "become
one flesh" (Gen 2:24; Mt 19:5–6). The tradition has read this union of
the spouses in the conjugal act as the proper place where new life is to
begin.[^22] The Church's teaching on the transmission of life rests on
exactly this point. The unitive and procreative meanings of the conjugal
act are inseparable.[^23] The child has a right "to be conceived,
carried in the womb, brought into the world and brought up within
marriage". Procreation is to be "the fruit of the conjugal act specific to
the love between the spouses".[^24]

The core therefore says that, among creatures, the child *ought* to come
from the spouses as one principle, united in the conjugal act. The modal
word matters, and the formal statement got it wrong at first. In God the
unity of principle is a necessity. Among creatures it is a norm.
Children are conceived outside marriage and outside the conjugal act, and
when they are, nothing is lacking in the child. The magisterium says
exactly this. The same instruction that judges a technique illicit insists
that every child so conceived "must in any case be accepted as a living gift
of the divine Goodness".[^25] The norm binds the acts of the parents. It
grades no one's dignity. This distinction between what *cannot* be in God
and what *ought not* to be among creatures is the analogy of being applied
to a particular case, and the next section returns to it.

A further precision concerns the bond. One might think that marriage
itself, the standing union of the spouses, is what makes them one principle
of a child, so that any child of married parents comes from them as one
principle however it was conceived. The core denies this, with Pius XII and
with *Donum vitae*. The union in question belongs to the conjugal *act*, the
spouses' personal and bodily self-giving, not merely to the *state* of
being married. The bond is necessary but not sufficient. It confers a right
to the conjugal act, not a right to a child: "the child is not an object to
which one has a right".[^26] This is the axiom on which the core's
derivation of *Donum vitae* depends, and it has a warrant earlier than
*Donum vitae* itself: the addresses of Pius XII of 1951 and 1956, and
*Humanae vitae*.[^27]

### 6. The measure of the analogy

Everything said so far would be dangerous without the rule that governs
all speech about God and creatures together. The Fourth Lateran Council
stated it: "between the Creator and the creature no likeness can be noted
without a greater unlikeness having to be noted between them".[^28]

The core keeps this rule in three ways.

First, *nothing is said of God and creatures in the same way*. A perfection
may be said of both. It is said first of God, from whom it is received, and
of creatures by participation.[^29] Its mode differs on each side. Unity
of principle is a necessity in God and a norm in creatures. Origin is
eternal procession in God and temporal generation in creatures. The two are
one principle by identity of power in God, and by a union of persons in an act
in creatures. If the mode were made the same on both sides, one of two
errors would follow at once. Either the creaturely norm becomes a
necessity, and a child conceived outside marriage becomes impossible,
which is false. Or the divine necessity becomes a norm, and God is placed
under a law, which is worse.

This is also the place to locate the old dispute between St Thomas and Bl.
John Duns Scotus about whether being and the pure perfections are said of
God and creatures *analogically* or *univocally*. Scotus held that there is
a concept of being common to God and creatures, but he held equally that
the *intrinsic mode* of that being, infinite in God and finite in creatures,
differs absolutely.[^30] What the core requires is that the *mode* never be
the same on both sides, and on that point Thomas and Scotus agree. The
contemporary charge that Scotus's univocity of *concept* leads by itself to
a univocity of *mode*, and so to a God who is merely the largest being, is
disputed by Scotus's best readers.[^31] The core does not settle that
dispute and does not need to.

Second, *God is not a member of any order of creatures*. God is not in a
genus.[^32] He is not the top of a hierarchy, the largest of beings, or the
first link of a chain. Robert Sokolowski called this the Christian
distinction: God together with the world is not greater than God
alone.[^33] Any scheme that sets God at the summit of an ascending series
of levels has already lost the distinction, however devout its intention.

Third, *no creaturely relation may be read back into God unchanged*. Among
the relations the core admits, the relation of spouses, a man and a woman
giving themselves to each other in a bodily union, has no place in God.
The Father and the Son are one principle of the Spirit by one power, not by
a union of two. To describe the Trinity as a marriage, or the Spirit as the
child of a divine couple, is to read a creaturely likeness into God without
the unlikeness. St Augustine refused this when he examined, and rejected,
the proposal that the family of man, woman and child is the image of the
Trinity.[^34] He preferred the image of the mind knowing and loving itself.

Here the core reaches a conclusion that it can state with some precision.
Neither image is complete, and they fail in opposite directions. The family
offers three persons in communion, but no relation in it corresponds to
the processions in God. The child is not to the parents as the Spirit is to
the Father and the Son. The mind, knowing itself in a word and loving
itself in that knowledge, offers two processions after the manner of
intellect and will, as St Thomas showed.[^35] But word and love in us are
acts, not persons. One image keeps the persons and loses the processions;
the other keeps the processions and loses the persons. Each supplies what
the other lacks, and neither is complete. That is just what Lateran IV
requires of every likeness. It also explains why the Church can speak, as
St John Paul II did, of man as the image of God "in the communion of
persons" without contradicting Augustine.[^36] The two images are not
rivals but partial reflections of a mystery that exceeds both. If someone
proposed an image complete in every respect, so that the mind's acts were
persons, the analogy would have collapsed into identity, and the proposal
could be rejected on that ground alone.

## Part Three: Gift and Communion

### 7. Consent: receiving is an act

The core holds that a person receives a gift of self only by consent. The
receiving is itself an act, not a mere passivity, and no one can perform it
on another's behalf.

The paradigm is the Annunciation. The Incarnation waited on the consent of
the Virgin, which, St Thomas says, was sought "in place of the whole of
human nature".[^37] The Council says the same: the Father willed that the
Incarnation be preceded by the acceptance of her who was predestined to be
the Mother.[^38] Mary's *fiat* is an act of the highest order, a free
receiving that makes possible the greatest of all gifts.

Canon law states the same principle for marriage. Marriage is brought into
being by the consent of the parties, "which no human power is able to
supply".[^39] Neither parents, nor state, nor Church can consent on a
person's behalf, because what is given and received is the person.

There is one apparent exception, and it confirms the rule. An infant is
baptized before it can consent, and the Church holds that the infant truly
receives the grace of the sacrament. St Thomas explains that little
children believe "through others", in the faith of the Church.[^40] The
consent is not dispensed with. It is held in trust by the Church, to be
made the child's own as it grows into the faith. The core admits this one
case of consent held in trust. It admits it because the Church gives it, and
only where the Church gives it. When a third party claims to supply the consent
of a capable adult to marriage, the law answers that the marriage is null.

### 8. The conjugal bond

The core defines the bond of marriage by two things: the mutual consent of
the spouses, and their capacity for the act proper to marriage. Both are
canonical.

The consent is described by the Code in the language of gift: "an act of
the will by which a man and a woman mutually give and accept each other
through an irrevocable covenant".[^41] The act proper to marriage is the
conjugal act "per se suitable for the procreation of offspring … by which
the spouses become one flesh". A marriage is consummated by it.[^42]

A clear canonical result follows, and it is perhaps the best illustration
of what the core claims to do. The Code holds that antecedent and perpetual
*impotence* invalidates marriage, while *sterility* neither prohibits nor
invalidates it.[^43] The difference is not arbitrary. The bond is a mutual
gift of persons, completed in an act of bodily self-giving. A person
incapable of that act cannot give what marriage consists in. A sterile
person can give it entirely. Fertility is not part of what is given. It is
a further good to which the act is ordered and which God may or may not
grant. The core derives the line the Code draws from the nature of the gift
without mentioning fertility at all. It does not claim that this reading is
new. It belongs to the canonical tradition, and in recent decades it has
been developed most fully by the school of Navarra and by Cormac Burke.[^44]
What the core contributes is the demonstration that the same structure also
yields the doctrine of the Trinity and the theology of the image.

### 9. Spousal love and friendship

The tradition knows more than one form of love between persons, and the
core distinguishes two of them by their structure rather than by their
intensity.

*Spousal* love is the love of persons turned toward each other in a total
and exclusive gift of self, sealed by a bond and expressed in the conjugal
act. *Friendship* is the love of persons turned together toward a common
good: truth, virtue, a shared work, finally God. Aristotle saw that perfect
friendship is a partnership in the good life.[^45] St Aelred wrote a whole
treatise on spiritual friendship as a way to God.[^46] C. S. Lewis made the
same distinction memorably: lovers stand face to face, absorbed in each
other, while friends stand side by side, absorbed in some common
interest.[^47] St Thomas took friendship as the model for charity itself,
the friendship of man with God founded on God's sharing of his
beatitude.[^48]

The core's rule is that the conjugal act belongs to spousal love alone,
that is, within marriage. No intensity of affection between friends makes
their friendship spousal. This is the Church's teaching on chastity, stated
as a distinction of kinds.[^49] It also protects friendship. If every
strong love were suspected of being a disguised eros, the tradition of
spiritual friendship, from David and Jonathan to Aelred and beyond, would
become unintelligible. The core holds the two apart so that each can be what
it is.

### 10. Totality and uniqueness

A finite person who gives himself *totally* can do so to one person only.
This is not a moral rule added to the nature of marriage. It follows from
what a total gift is. A finite whole given entirely to one is no longer
available to give entirely to another. The Church teaches the unity and
indissolubility of marriage as its essential properties, and St John Paul
II explained the incompatibility of polygamy with conjugal communion in
just these terms: it "radically contradicts" a communion in which each gives
himself wholly.[^50]

God's gift is different, and the difference is instructive. God gives
himself wholly to each without being divided or diminished. The whole
Christ is present under each species and under every part of each.[^51] As
the sequence for Corpus Christi puts it, *sumit unus, sumunt mille*: one
receives, a thousand receive, and each receives as much as all.[^52] St
Augustine said that God cares for each of us "as if he cared for that one
alone, and for all as for each".[^53] Totality implies uniqueness for a
finite giver, not for an infinite one. Here the analogy of being serves as
a rule of reasoning. An argument from the uniqueness of the marriage bond to
any limitation of God's self-gift, or from God's self-gift to a plurality
of spouses, fails at the same point.

### 11. The bridal vocation of every person

Scripture and the mystical tradition speak of the soul, and of the Church,
as the Bride of Christ. From Origen's homilies on the Song of Songs to St
Bernard's sermons and beyond, this has been one of the great themes of
Christian prayer.[^54] St John Paul II gave it a precise anthropological
form. In the Church every human being, male and female, is the Bride "in
that he or she accepts the gift of the love of Christ the Redeemer, and
seeks to respond to it with the gift of his or her own person".[^55]

The words "in that he or she accepts" matter. The bridal relation to
Christ is offered to every person, and every person is called to it. It is
*realized* in acceptance. The formal core once omitted this condition and
said simply that every person stands in the bridal relation to Christ.
Stated that way, the axiom made the refusal of the gift unintelligible. It
also, as it turned out, silently excluded a theological opinion the Church
has protected: that infants who die without baptism and without
personal sin attain a natural happiness without the vision of God.[^56] The
correction came from the axiom's own source. It was enough to read the
sentence of *Mulieris dignitatem* to its end. The bridal vocation is
universal. The bridal relation is a response.

### 12. Image and dignity without degrees

Every human person is made in the image of God (Gen 1:27), and the image is
not possessed in degrees.[^57] The core draws four consequences, and each
rests on a point of doctrine.

*Origin does not rank.* The Son is from the Father and is his equal. Nicaea
excluded once and for all the inference from being-from-another to being
less.[^58] The same holds analogically among creatures. A child is not less
than its parents, and a wife is not less than her husband, because of an
order of origin or of receiving.[^59]

*Capacity does not rank.* The image does not depend on the exercise of
reason. The Word was made flesh at the first instant of conception, and
Christ's body was formed and animated in that instant.[^60] The embryonic
Christ was no less the image of the Father than the risen Christ. Every
human embryo is a person in the image of God, whatever it can or cannot yet
do.

*Marriage does not complete the image.* Christ, "the image of the invisible
God" (Col 1:15), was unmarried. The image is complete in each person, and
the communion of persons in marriage is one way, not the only way, in which
it is lived.[^61]

*States of life do not rank persons.* All the faithful, in every state, are
called to the fullness of holiness.[^62] Here the core must be stated
precisely, because the formal statement first stated it too broadly. The
Council of Trent teaches, under anathema, that it is "better and more
blessed" to remain in virginity or celibacy than to marry.[^63] St John Paul
II reaffirmed that the Church "has always defended the superiority of this
charism to that of marriage, by reason of the wholly singular link which it
has with the Kingdom of God".[^64] The core does not and may not deny this.
What it denies is something else: that the gift of self is *greater* in one
state than in another, or that persons are ranked by their state. The same
passage of *Familiaris consortio* calls virginity and marriage "two ways of
expressing and living the one mystery of the covenant". It adds that
virginity presupposes and confirms the dignity of marriage.[^65] In each
state the gift of self is total. The excellence of consecrated virginity
lies in what it signifies: its eschatological witness and its undivided
heart (1 Cor 7:32–35). It does not lie in a larger quantity of self-giving.
The formal core has been corrected to say exactly this. Trent's teaching
now sits beside the corrected prohibition without conflict.

## Part Four: Authority and Law

### 13. Grades of certitude

Theology has long distinguished the authority with which a teaching is
proposed from its content. The manuals assigned *theological notes*: *de
fide definita*, *proxima fidei*, *theologice certa*, *sententia communis*,
and so on, each with a corresponding censure for its denial.[^66] The
Council and the Code have since restated the grades in terms of the assent
owed. Divine and Catholic faith is owed to what is revealed and proposed as
such. Firm assent is owed to what is definitively proposed. Religious
submission of intellect and will is owed to the authentic ordinary
magisterium.[^67] *Donum veritatis* explained how a theologian should
receive teachings at each level, including those of a prudential
character.[^68]

The checker's authority layer is a codification of this inheritance, and
three of its features deserve explicit theological statement.

*Only a definition closes a question.* Where the Church has defined, the
contrary opinion is excluded. Where she teaches with lesser authority,
the contrary opinion is not thereby annihilated. It is placed in a
subordinate position, from which a later development could, in principle,
recall it. The checker keeps such opinions on record with a note of what
would restore them. It does so because the history of doctrine shows that
the Church herself has done this.

*The Church protects disputed schools.* The clearest case is the Immaculate
Conception. For three centuries the weight of authority among the
theologians, including several later Doctors, ran against the doctrine
that Bl. John Duns Scotus defended. Sixtus IV forbade either party to accuse
the other of heresy, and Trent declined to include the Blessed Virgin in
its decree on original sin.[^69] The minority opinion survived because it
was protected, not because it was weighty. In 1854 it was defined.[^70]
The same protection kept both schools alive in the controversy *de auxiliis*
after 1607. Neither was ever condemned.[^71] The lesson for any method that
weighs authorities is sobering. The count of authorities on one side
predicts nothing. Development of doctrine, in Newman's sense, proceeds
"in the same dogma, the same sense, the same meaning", but not by majority
vote.[^72]

*Form, not author, fixes the grade.* A pope teaches infallibly only when he
defines *ex cathedra*, with all the conditions the First Vatican Council
laid down. Most papal acts are exercises of the ordinary magisterium or of
legislative power.[^73] A document's grade cannot be raised by a
commentary on it. Nor can a claim about the extent of the definitive
magisterium be settled by an authority lower than the one whose extent is
in question.

### 14. Law in the service of doctrine

The law of the Church is not a second source of doctrine. St John Paul II
described the Code of 1983 as an attempt to "translate" the ecclesiology
of the Council "into canonical language".[^74] The Code itself ends by
naming its purpose: "the salvation of souls, which must always be the
supreme law in the Church".[^75] Law presupposes doctrine and serves it.
When the Code requires divine and Catholic faith, firm assent, or
religious submission, it attaches an obligation to what is taught at each
grade.[^76] It teaches nothing itself.

The checker therefore treats teaching and law as an ordered pair: doctrine
first, law ordered to it. Two rules follow. A canon whose application
presupposes a doctrinal judgment must not be applied where that judgment is
denied. And no conclusion of law may be used to settle a question of
doctrine. These are not innovations. They are the old distinction between
the power of teaching and the power of governance, applied with some care.

Within its own domain, canon law has a different logic from theology.
Theology weighs. Law, once the facts are established and the norm in force
identified, *determines*. An act is valid or invalid, a penalty incurred or
not, a faculty possessed or lacking. The Code supplies its own hermeneutics.
The legislator interprets his law authentically. Penal laws and laws
restricting rights are interpreted strictly. Where the law is silent, a
case is resolved from similar laws, general principles, the practice of the
Curia, and "the common and constant opinion of learned persons", except in
penal matters.[^77] The opinion of canonists fills a gap in the law. It
never weighs against the law. The checker learned this the hard way: its
first attempt to encode a contemporary canonical question treated it as if it
were a theological controversy, and a canonist rightly objected.

The interior forum is beyond the reach of both. Whether a person is
culpable, what he intended, what he believed: these belong to God and to the
confessor. The external forum may *presume* imputability once an external
violation is established. It may never *read* a conscience.[^78] The
checker accepts such facts only as given, never as computed. That is not a
technical limitation. It is the right ordering of the discipline.

## Conclusion: what the core can and cannot do

A formal core of this kind does one thing well: it keeps an argument
honest. When every term must be given a sort, and every relation a kind,
equivocations that pass unnoticed in prose cannot be stated without
contradiction. The core has found several such equivocations in its own
first statement. It said "cannot" where it should have said "ought not". It
said "is" where its source said "is called to be". It forbade a ranking
that the Church teaches, because it had not distinguished the excellence of
a state from the measure of a gift. Each correction came from the tradition
itself, and each made the core more faithful to it.

What a formal core cannot do is show that a well-formed claim is true. It
can show that a proposal is incoherent with what the Church teaches. It can
show that a proposal follows from what she teaches, given certain readings.
It can show what each reading costs. The judgment of truth belongs to
Scripture, Tradition and the magisterium, which "are so linked and joined
together that one cannot stand without the others".[^79] The core claims
only fittingness: that the structure of giving and receiving, set in its
proper analogical order, illuminates the Trinity, the image of God in man,
and the law of marriage together. Whether it does is for the reader to
judge.

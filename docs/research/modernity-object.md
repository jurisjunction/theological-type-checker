# The moral object, from Thomas to the proportionalists: the evidence record for a ninth part, (formal, will)

This is the research record for a ninth part of TTC-MODERNITY, (formal, will): the formal cause of the will's act. It was compiled on 24 October 2026. It is an evidence record, so it keeps prose (rule 10). Nothing here is encoded; the values are proposals. The part is called `form_of_act` below, a neutral name, since Thomas's form and Kant's are not the same form (Proposed values, consequence 1).

In review the part was split in two (the predictions, THE DESIGN): `object_as_form`, (formal, will), the object of the chosen act, which is `form_of_act` below save at Kant; and `form_of_law`, (formal, reason), the form of the law, which Kant alone holds. Kant excludes the first and adds the second.

## Corrections to the brief, kept because they shape the encoding

1. **"sicut forma" is not Thomas's phrase.** I-II q. 18 a. 2 has "Sicut autem res naturalis habet speciem ex sua forma, ita actio habet speciem ex obiecto" [V]. The object as form is in ad 2, and qualified: it "habet quodammodo rationem formae, inquantum dat speciem" [V]. The object is form by a proportion, "in a way".
2. **In Thomas the end is the formal part of the whole human act.** "actus humani species formaliter consideratur secundum finem, materialiter autem secundum obiectum exterioris actus" (q. 18 a. 6) [V]. The object is the form of the exterior act, and the end is the object of the interior act. So the part must say which act it names. VS 78 joins them: the object is "the proximate end of a deliberate decision" [V].
3. **Kant's form is defined against the object.** "By the matter of a practical principle I mean the object of the will" (KpV §4) [V; the § as M], and every material principle falls "under the general principle of self-love or private happiness" [V]. One part for Thomas's form and Kant's makes two contrary forms one cause.
4. **KpV §4 is printed in Abbott as "IV. THEOREM II."** [V]. AA 5:27 is [M].
5. **Ockham's loci.** Neither Pelletier (SEP) nor Freddoso cites Quodl. I q. 20 or Quodl. III q. 14 [V, both pages]. Freddoso cites OTh VIII 335, 394; OTh V 352–353; OTh IX 177 [S]. The brief's loci are [M].
6. **Scotus: the object gives generic goodness, not moral species.** Wolter's Scotus: "an act is said to receive its generic goodness from its object"; "Not that the nature of its object determines its moral species" [S: Shannon 1993, quoting Wolter, *Will and Morality* 215]. Moral goodness is the act's full conformity with right reason.
7. **Abelard's tag "non enim quae fiunt, sed quo animo fiant, pensat Deus"** was not found in any source fetched [M].
8. **Knauer's English is "commensurate reason".** The first form is *Nouvelle revue théologique* 87 (1965) 356–76; the English is *Natural Law Forum* (1967), from p. 132 [V, the article's footnote]. The volume number, 12, is [M].
9. **Kant on self-preservation.** In the Doctrine of Virtue, self-preservation "in itself can establish no duty" [V, praxeology.net, in the section on natural perfection; AA locus M]. The duty not to kill oneself rests on "the subject of morality in his own person" [V].

## Thomas Aquinas (the baseline)

**Species from the object.**
- "Sicut autem res naturalis habet speciem ex sua forma, ita actio habet speciem ex obiecto; sicut et motus ex termino" (I-II q. 18 a. 2) [V, Latin, liriocatolico.com.br]. "just as a natural thing has its species from its form, so an action has its species from its object" [V, New Advent].
- "prima bonitas actus moralis attenditur ex obiecto convenienti" [V]; "the primary goodness of a moral action is derived from its suitable object" [V].
- Ad 2: "obiectum non est materia ex qua, sed materia circa quam, et habet quodammodo rationem formae" [V]. English: it "stands in relation to the act as its form, as it were" [V].

**The fourfold goodness (a. 4).** "Sic igitur in actione humana bonitas quadruplex considerari potest" [V]:
- "Una quidem secundum genus, prout scilicet est actio";
- "Alia vero secundum speciem, quae accipitur secundum obiectum conveniens";
- "Tertia secundum circumstantias, quasi secundum accidentia quaedam";
- "Quarta autem secundum finem, quasi secundum habitudinem ad causam bonitatis" [V].

**Measured by reason (a. 5).** "bonum et malum dicitur per comparationem ad rationem" [V]. The difference of good and evil "circa obiectum considerata, comparatur per se ad rationem" [V]. "Essentialis" is in ad 4, of a circumstance: "circumstantia quandoque sumitur ut differentia essentialis obiecti" [V]. So the object's form is reason's measure, not the will's.

**End and object (a. 6).**
- "actus humani species formaliter consideratur secundum finem, materialiter autem secundum obiectum exterioris actus" [V].
- "the interior act of the will takes its species from the end, as from its own proper object" [V, New Advent].
- Aristotle's thief: "ille qui furatur ut committat adulterium, est, per se loquendo, magis adulter quam fur" [V].

**Indifference and circumstance.**
- Some acts are indifferent in species: "to pick up a straw from the ground, to walk in the fields" (a. 8) [V, New Advent].
- A circumstance "can again be taken by the directing reason, as the principal condition of the object that determines the action's species" (a. 10) [V, New Advent]. Its example: "to steal from a holy place" [V]. The Latin of a. 10 was not reached [M].

**Interior and exterior act (q. 20 aa. 1–2).**
- The goodness the exterior act has "secundum se, propter debitam materiam et debitas circumstantias", "non derivatur a voluntate, sed magis a ratione" (a. 1) [V].
- "Et illa quidem quae est secundum ordinem ad finem, tota dependet ex voluntate" (a. 2) [V].
- "Illa autem quae est ex debita materia vel circumstantiis, dependet ex ratione" [V].
- "ad hoc autem quod sit simpliciter bonum, non sufficit unum singulare bonum, sed requiritur integritas bonitatis" [V].

**Species from the end (q. 1 a. 3).**
- "ea quae sunt composita ex materia et forma, constituuntur in suis speciebus per proprias formas" [V].
- Human acts, "sive considerentur per modum actionum, sive per modum passionum, a fine speciem sortiuntur" [V].
- Ad 3: one act "non ordinatur nisi ad unum finem proximum, a quo habet speciem" [V].

**Double effect (II-II q. 64 a. 7)**, which the proportionalists read.
- "moral acts take their species according to what is intended, and not according to what is beside the intention" [V, New Advent].
- An act from a good intention "may be rendered unlawful, if it be out of proportion to the end" [V]. The Latin page failed [M].

**Readings in the literature.**
- Hoffmann: Aquinas takes "the end (that which is willed) to be not only the origin of moral goodness, but also its main criterion" [S: abstract, PhilPapers HOFMAA; journal and year M].
- Another abstract: the exterior act "is both the proximate end and the object of the interior act" [S: abstract, PhilPapers OSBTGA; author M].

**Holds (formal, will)? Yes.** The object is the form of the exterior act and the proximate end of the interior act. The further end is (final, will), already held as `ends_desired`. Goodness needs both ("integritas bonitatis"), so an argument from the object alone is typed (formal, will), and one from object and end is typed conjunctively.

**Tongue.** "quodammodo rationem formae" is carried by the Dominican Fathers' "as its form, as it were" [V]. The comparison is one of proportion: object is to action as form to a natural thing, and as term to motion. No encoding makes it univocal.

## Peter Abelard (background)

- "the agent's intention alone determines the moral worth of an action"; "in themselves deeds are morally indifferent" [S: Arlig and King, SEP "Peter Abelard", §6].
- Sin is consent. "Sometimes we sin without any bad will at all": the servant who kills his master under duress [S: Wikipedia, "Ethics (Abelard)", translator unnamed].
- The Council of Sens (1140) condemned him "for suggesting that actions were 'morally indifferent'" [S: Wikipedia]. The DH numbers are [M].
- Hoffmann reads his ethics as "non-subjectivist", with a theory of the end like Thomas's [S: abstract].

**Holds (formal, will)? No, as background.** No node is proposed. He shows the definitional point: an ethics of intention alone still specifies the interior act by what it consents to. So the part must name the chosen act, not any act of will.

## John Duns Scotus

**The object and moral goodness.**
- The object gives "generic goodness"; "Not that the nature of its object determines its moral species" [S: Shannon, *TS* 54 (1993) 283–84, quoting Wolter, *Will and Morality* 215].
- Moral goodness is relational: "just as beauty is not some absolute quality in a beautiful body"; it is "a kind of decoration it has" (Ord. I d. 17 n. 62) [S: Shannon n. 44, through Ingham]. The Latin "quasi decor" is [M].
- It "consists in its having all that the agent's right reason declares" (Quodl. 18 n. 3) [S: Shannon n. 50]. Shannon cites the same sentence later as "Ordinatio I, q. 18", so his loci are inconsistent [S].
- "according to right reason it is becoming to the act" (Ord. II d. 40) [S: Shannon n. 43, Wolter 227].
- The object is "a true source of moral, albeit generic, good or bad"; "Abraham's act would be bad on account of its object" [S: Santogrossi, *TS* 55 (1994), on Quodl. 18, *God and Creatures* 400–405].
- For Albert and Scotus "the end is accidental to the moral act" [S: Hoffmann, abstract].

**The voluntarist pole.**
- "no act can be morally good if it runs counter to a precept the observance of which is required by God" (Quodl. 18 n. 7) [S: Santogrossi, *God and Creatures* 415].
- "[N]ulla lex est recta nisi quatenus a voluntate divina acceptante est statuta" (Ord. I d. 44 q. 1 n. 8) [S: Santogrossi, Wolter 256].
- The second table is natural law only in a looser sense (Ord. III d. 37) (record: [S]).

**Holds (formal, will)?**
- `scotus_dpp`: **yes**, by inheritance from `scotist`. Warrant: Quodl. 18, the object as generic goodness and moral goodness as right reason's full dictate [S]. The form is a relation of fit to right reason, not the object alone.
- `scotus_will`: **no**. What is right is right as accepted by the divine will (Ord. I d. 44 n. 8 [S]; Ord. III d. 37 [S]). A reading, as the record's will readings are.

## William of Ockham

- "It is a will-based ethics in which intentions, that is, free and voluntary acts of willing, count for everything" [S: Pelletier, SEP "William of Ockham", §7].
- "External behavior or actions are morally neutral in themselves" [S: Pelletier, §7]. They take their worth from the intentions behind them [S].
- "Morally good" applies "directly to interior acts of willing or willing-against that are intrinsically good", and "only indirectly or by 'extrinsic denomination'" to other acts [S: Freddoso, "Ockham", no locus].
- An act is intrinsically good only if "it has as part of its intentional object the motive of conforming one's will to the dictates of right reason" [S: Freddoso, no locus].
- "Given the ordination that is now in force, no act is perfectly virtuous unless it is elicited in conformity with right reason" (OTh VIII 394) [S: Freddoso].
- God is free to prohibit "even the very act of loving God" (OTh V 352–353) [S: Freddoso].
- Positive moral science concerns acts "neither good nor evil except because it is prohibited or commanded by a superior" (OTh IX 177) [S: Freddoso].
- The intrinsically virtuous act: "loving God above all else and for his own sake" [S: Pelletier, §7.1, citing *On the Connection of the Virtues* without pages].
- Quodl. I q. 20 (the exterior act adds no goodness) and Quodl. III q. 14 (an act necessarily virtuous) are [M].

**Holds (formal, will)? No.** The exterior act, as chosen, has no moral species of its own; its goodness is an extrinsic denomination from the will. Warrant: Pelletier and Freddoso [S]; Quodl. I q. 20 [M]. `ockham_will` and `ockham_nominal` inherit the exclusion.

**Caution.** On Freddoso's reading the interior act's intrinsic goodness comes from its intentional object. Under a part defined loosely, as any act's form from its object, Ockham would hold it for acts of will. Defined as the chosen act's object (VS 78), he does not.

## Francisco Suárez

- Some acts of will are evil "ex se, et ex vi suorum obiectorum ... ante omnem voluntatem prohibentem" (*De bonitate et malitia*, tr. 3 disp. 7 sect. 1 n. 6; Vivès IV 373) [S: Polgar, *De Ethica* 5.1 (2018) 23]. Polgar's English: "in themselves and in their objects evil prior to any will prohibiting them" [S].
- "Nam quidam habent adiunctam malitiam ex vi directae, et physicae tendentiae in obiectum" (n. 13, Vivès IV 375) [S: Polgar].
- "fornicationem esse intrinsece malam, usuram esse injustam" (*De legibus* II.7 n. 5, Vivès V 113) [S: Polgar].
- Polgar reads his "intrinsic" as "seemingly nothing but a reference to a judgement of right reason" [S].
- Natural law is both indicative of good and evil and a divine prohibition, against Gregory of Rimini and against Ockham (*De legibus* II.6 nn. 3–5, 13) (record: [V, Freddoso translation]).
- *De bonitate et malitia humanorum actuum* is in Vivès vol. 4, archive.org `rpfranciscisuare04suar`, from p. 277 [V: Penner, "Suárez in Latin online"]. Its text was not read. Its account of "bonitas obiectiva" (disp. 2) is [M].

**Holds (formal, will)? Yes, by his own add.** `suarez_dm23` has `suarezian` and `scotus_dpp`, which hold the part, and `ockham`, which excludes it. So adding the part raises a clash there. Suárez resolves it with `add`: the object makes the act evil before any prohibiting will (*De bonitate*, disp. 7 sect. 1 n. 6 [S]; *De legibus* II.6 (record: [V])). This is the documented clash "Suárez, on natural law", added in revision, so it is not independent.

## Thomas Hobbes

- "But whatsoever is the object of any man's appetite or desire, that is it which he for his part calleth good" (*Leviathan* 6) [V, Standard Ebooks, partly modernized].
- "nothing simply and absolutely so; nor any common rule of good and evil, to be taken from the nature of the objects themselves" [V].
- The rule is taken "from the person of the man", "from the person that representeth it", or "from an arbitrator or judge" [V].
- "Will, therefore, is the last appetite in deliberating." [V].
- "Good, and Evill, are names that signifie our Appetites, and Aversions;" (*Leviathan* 15) [V, pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca, original spelling].
- "Morall Philosophy is nothing else but the Science of what is Good, and Evill, in the conversation, and Society of mankind" [V].
- The laws of nature are "but Conclusions, or Theoremes", and laws properly as "delivered in the word of God" [V].
- Private judgement of good and evil is true "in the condition of meer Nature, where there are no Civill Lawes"; otherwise "the measure of Good and Evill actions, is the Civill Law" (*Leviathan* 29) [V].

**Holds (formal, will)? No.** Warrant: *Leviathan* 6, "nor any common rule of good and evil, to be taken from the nature of the objects themselves" [V].

**The clash.** `default`, `bacon` and `descartes` hold the part; only `ockham`*, the contested edge, excludes it. So the clash at `hobbes` on the new part rests on that edge alone. Hobbes resolves it by `exclude`. Without the edge (EX1) there is no clash, and the exclusion is his own move.

**Tongue.** Hobbes's "object" is the thing appetite tends to. Thomas's *obiectum* is the act's "materia circa quam" as reason apprehends it. One English word carries two meanings, and the Dominican Fathers' Thomas uses it too. The Latin *Leviathan* (1668) at ch. 6 was not checked [M]. The denial, "the nature of the objects themselves", reaches both meanings. A reading that makes Hobbes answer Thomas there rests on the shared word, and is a claim with its claimant.

## Baruch Spinoza

- Good and bad "indicate no positive quality in things regarded in themselves", "but are merely modes of thinking" (*Ethics* IV Praef.) [V, Elwes, Gutenberg]. Music "is good for him that is melancholy, bad for him that mourns" [V].
- "By good I mean that which we certainly know to be useful to us." (IV Def. 1) [V]. Evil is "a hindrance to us in the attainment of any good" (Def. 2) [V].
- "By an end, for the sake of which we do something, I mean a desire." (Def. 7) [V].
- "By virtue (virtus) and power I mean the same thing" (Def. 8) [V].
- "To act absolutely in obedience to virtue is in us the same thing as to act, to live, or to preserve one's being" (IV p24) [V].
- "But no action, considered in itself alone, is either good or bad (as we pointed out in the preface to Pt. IV.)" (IV p59, alternative proof) [V].
- "A given action is called bad, in so far as it arises from one being affected by hatred or any evil emotion." [V].
- "The action of striking, in so far as it is considered physically", "is a virtue or excellence which is conceived as proper to the structure of the human body" (p59 schol.) [V]. "one and the same action can be associated with various mental images of things" [V].
- The Latin, "nulla actio in se sola considerata bona aut mala est", is [M].

**Holds (formal, will)? No.** Warrant: IV p59, alternative proof [V]; IV Praef. [V]. An action's goodness comes from its cause in the mind (adequate idea or passion), not from its object.

**The clash (new).** `descartes` holds the part and `hobbes` excludes it, so it joins Spinoza's clash. He resolves it by `exclude`, on IV p59.

**Note.** The striking example is the record's nearest text to Thomas's one act in "natural species" ordered to justice or to anger (q. 1 a. 3 ad 3). Spinoza keeps the one physical act and denies it a moral species. Knauer keeps the same premise ("The purely physical series of events is irrelevant", below) and moves the species to the commensurate reason. Bentham's "act itself" is the same move a third time.

## John Locke

- Conformity of voluntary actions to a rule is a relation "which, I think, may be called moral relation" (*Essay* II.xxviii.4) [V, oregonstate.edu].
- "Moral good and evil, then, is only the conformity or disagreement of our voluntary actions to some law" (§5) [V].
- That good and evil is "drawn on us, from the will and power of the law-maker"; it is "reward and punishment" [V].
- "It would be in vain for one intelligent being to set a rule to the actions of another" without power to reward and punish (§6) [V].
- Three laws: "The divine law", "The civil law", "The law of opinion or reputation" (§7) [V]. The divine law "is the only true touchstone of moral rectitude" (§8) [V].
- "their rectitude or obliquity consists in the agreement or disagreement with those patterns prescribed by some law" (§14) [V].
- "there is often no distinction made between the positive idea of the action, and the reference it has to a rule" (§16) [V]. His example: taking a madman's sword is called stealing, yet is no sin by God's law [V, the tool's summary].
- The mind judges "comparing the action with the rule" (§20) [V].
- Morality as demonstrable (IV.iii.18) and mixed modes as made by the mind (III.v) are [M].

**Holds (formal, will)? No.** Warrant: II.xxviii.5, 8, 16 [V]. An action's moral quality is a relation to a lawgiver's law with sanctions. Its "positive idea" has no moral species.

**The clash (new).** `hooker`, `descartes` and `grotius` hold the part; `hobbes`* excludes it. Locke resolves it by `exclude`. On the moral object he sides with Hobbes against Hooker and Grotius, and puts the act's moral form in its relation to the Maker's law. Together with his own add of `ends_by_god_ethics`, that is one move: the measure is the divine lawgiver. Without the Hobbes edge there is no clash, and the exclusion is his own.

**A reading left for later.** If Locke's morality is demonstrable from mixed modes whose real essence is the nominal one, the act-kind has a form after all, made by the mind. A sibling `locke_demonstrative` would hold the part [M].

## Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

- Against "certain philosophers" for whom the eternal truths, "and consequently the principles of goodness", are effects of God's will (*Discourse* §2) (record: [V, Montgomery]).
- The Euthyphro question, whether "it is good and just because God wills it or whether God wills it because it is good and just" [S: Horn, IEP "Leibniz: Law and Justice"].
- Justice rests "on eternal truths, objects of the divine intellect" (opinion on Pufendorf) [S: Horn].
- "God does not act through absolute power alone, without reason, as would a tyrant" (to Molanus) [S: Horn].
- "*Charity* is a universal benevolence" (to Nicaise, 1697) [S: Horn]. Justice as the charity of the wise is dated to the *Codex* of 1693 [S: Horn]; the preface as its locus is [M].
- His position is "a critical reaction to Hobbes"; he "fiercely criticized Pufendorf's voluntarism" [S: Horn].

**Holds (formal, will)? Yes.** `leibniz` inherits it from `suarez_dm23` and `descartes`, which both hold it, so there is no clash. Own warrant: *Discourse* §2 (record: [V]); the opinion on Pufendorf [S].

**Caution.** Leibniz has no doctrine of the object in Thomas's sense. His ethics of perfection and charity holds the part as anti-voluntarism: the act's goodness is from the divine understanding, not from command.

## Hugo Grotius

- "The law of nature is a dictate of right reason"; an act has "a quality of moral baseness or moral necessity" (DIBP I.1.10) [V, lonang.com, translator unnamed].
- "such an act is either forbidden or enjoined by the author of nature, God" [V].
- "The acts in regard to which such a dictate exists are, in themselves, either obligatory or not permissible" [V].
- "volitional divine law does not enjoin or forbid those things" that are obligatory "in themselves and by their own nature" [V].
- It "is unchangeable – even in the sense that it cannot be changed by God" [V]. "so He cannot cause that which is intrinsically evil be not evil" [V].
- Latin: "Jus naturale est dictatum rectae rationis, indicans actui alicui", "inesse moralem turpitudinem, aut necessitatem moralem" [S: Pizza, *Il Politico* 85.2 (2020), n. 3]. The rest of the Latin is [M].
- *Etiamsi daremus* (Prol. §11) (record: [M]).

**Holds (formal, will)? Yes.** `grotius` inherits it from `suarez_dm23`'s add; own warrant DIBP I.1.10 [V]. `grotius_etiamsi` holds it too: there the act's quality stands without God.

**Tongue.** "in themselves" in the English; "per se" in the Latin is [M]. The quality is from the act's agreement with rational nature, so a Grotian argument is typed (formal, will) together with the ends or form of human nature.

## Immanuel Kant

The texts. His node is set out under **Kant's node**.

**The form of the law** (*Groundwork*, Abbott, Gutenberg) [V]:
- "A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects" (4:394).
- An action from duty has its worth "not from the purpose which is to be attained by it, but from the maxim by which it is determined" (4:399–400).
- It is determined by "the formal principle of volition", every material principle being withdrawn (4:400).
- "the universal conformity of its actions to law in general"; "I am never to act otherwise than so that I could also will that my maxim should become a universal law" (4:402).
- German (4:402): "ich soll niemals anders verfahren, als so, dass ich auch wollen könne, meine Maxime solle ein allgemeines Gesetz werden" [V, Kirchmann's edition, Berlin 1870, archive.org OCR].
- "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (4:421).
- "Act as if the maxim of thy action were to become by thy will a universal law of nature." (4:421).
- "the objective principle of all practical legislation lies", "(according to the first principle) in the rule and its form of universality", "but the subjective principle is in the end" (4:431).

**The matter refused** (*Critique of Practical Reason*, Abbott, Gutenberg) [V]:
- "All material practical principles as such are of one and the same kind", and "come under the general principle of self-love or private happiness" (§3).
- A rational being can hold maxims as universal laws only as principles that determine the will "not by their matter, but by their form only" (§4, printed "IV. THEOREM II.").
- "By the matter of a practical principle I mean the object of the will." (§4; the § is M).
- The table of material principles names "Wolf and the Stoics" (perfection) and "Crusius and other theological Moralists" (God's will); "material principles are quite incapable of furnishing the supreme moral law".
- The principle of morality is "the formal supreme determining principle of the will".

**Heteronomy** (*Groundwork* 4:441–444, Abbott, Gutenberg plain text) [V]:
- Where the will seeks its law elsewhere, "there always results heteronomy"; the law is "given by the object through its relation to the will".
- The ontological concept of perfection is "empty and indefinite", yet "better than the theological conception which derives morality from a Divine absolutely perfect will".
- That divine will is combined "with the awful conceptions of might and vengeance".
- The principle of private happiness is "the most objectionable".

**Humanity as end in itself** (*Groundwork*, Abbott) [V]:
- "rational nature exists as an end in itself" (4:429).
- "treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other", "in every case as an end withal, never as means only" (4:429).
- Humanity is "an objective end, which must as a law constitute the supreme limiting condition of all our subjective ends" (4:431).
- "Autonomy then is the basis of the dignity of human and of every rational nature." (4:436).
- The end of a good will is "not as an end to be effected, but as an independently existing end" (4:437).
- The German "Zweck an sich selbst" at 4:428–429 was not reached [M].

**Ends in nature, God, the soul.**
- "The principle is regulative (not constitutive)"; "we cannot objectively establish the proposition: There is an intelligent original being;" (*Critique of Judgment* §75) [V, marxists.org, translator unnamed]. "we do not actually observe purposes in nature as intentional ones" [V]. §§61–68 are [M].
- The physico-theological proof (KrV A620/B648) and its conclusion, "an architect of the world ... not a creator" (A627/B655), are [M]. The Gutenberg Meiklejohn was refused and its fetched text stops early.
- The Paralogisms (A341/B399 ff.), against knowledge of the soul as substance, are [M].

**Natural ends and self-preservation in the Doctrine of Virtue** (praxeology.net, translator unnamed; AA loci M) [V]:
- "To destroy the subject of morality in his own person" is "obliterating from the world, as far as he can, the very existence of morality itself"; "but morality is, nevertheless, an end in itself."
- "to dispose of oneself as a mere means to some end of one�s own liking is to degrade the humaity in one�s own person" (the page's typos kept).
- "love of life is intended by nature for the preservation of his person"; "each is a natural end"; lust is "a desire contrary to an end of nature".
- "it is not so easy to produce a rational demonstration of the inadmissability of that unnatural use".
- "The ground of proof surely lies in the fact that a man gives up his personality (throws it away)".
- "For apart from the necessity of self-preservation," "which in itself can establish no duty," one owes it to oneself to be useful (natural perfection).
- The duty of self-preservation is the "first, though not principal, duty of a human being to himself as an animal being" (MS 6:421) [S: Lim, *Mudd Journal of Ethics*, citing Gregor].
- In the *Groundwork*'s suicide example, a nature with that law would "contradict itself", since the feeling exists to "impel to the improvement of life" (4:422) [V]. A natural purpose works inside the formula of the law of nature.

**Tongue.**
- Kant's pair is *Form* and *Materie*; Abbott's is "form" and "matter" [V]; the German at those places is [M].
- The pair is Thomas's, and the words cross over: for both, the object is matter ("materia circa quam"; "the object of the will"). Thomas makes it form "quodammodo", and Kant looks for the form elsewhere. Every encoding carries the words, and none says which use of "form" an argument rests on.
- "Zweck an sich selbst": the English "end in itself" carries *Zweck* as end, and the name is final in both tongues. The use is formal, a "limiting condition" [V]. No encoding decides the typing.

## The proportionalists and *Veritatis splendor*

**Knauer** (*Natural Law Forum*, 1967; first form NRT 87 (1965) 356–76) [V, scholarship.law.nd.edu PDF]:
- "Moral evil, I contend, consists in the last analysis in the permission or causing of a physical evil", one without commensurate reason (§I, p. 132).
- A physical evil "is not a moral evil, even if moral evil is definable only in relation to it" (§I).
- The *finis operis* is "the act which is willed and intended as such" (§II.1).
- "If the reason of an act is commensurate, it alone determines the finis operis, so that the act is morally good." (§IV).
- The reason is then "completely identical with the finis operis" (§V).
- "By direct is meant a causing or permission without a commensurate reason." (§XI).
- "The purely physical series of events is irrelevant to the moral qualification of good or bad." (§VII).
- "Neither the pure external happening nor the psychological intention is morally understandable alone." (§II.1).
- "a purely good intention in the psychological sense does not determine the moral goodness of an act." (§VIII).
- He reads II-II q. 64 a. 7: "moral acts receive their species according to what is intended" (§II.1; his footnote has "II-I").

**Schüller, Fuchs, McCormick, Janssens** [S: May, *Homiletic & Pastoral Review*, Dec. 1994, EWTN reprint]:
- Schüller: "The greater good is to be preferred."
- Fuchs: "we cannot judge an act in its 'materiality' without referring to the 'intention' of the agent".
- McCormick: the encyclical's account of proportionalism is a "misrepresentation"; "they do not hold or teach what the encyclical attributes to them".
- McCormick: "an action cannot be judged morally wrong simply by looking at 'the material happening'".
- May: proportionalists "redescribe the object chosen in terms of its hoped-for results". This is May's claim, against theirs.

***Veritatis splendor*** (6 August 1993):
- §74: "Certain ethical theories, called 'teleological', claim to be concerned for the conformity of human acts" [S: May].
- §75: consequentialism judges "solely from a calculation of foreseeable consequences"; proportionalism "focuses rather on the proportion acknowledged between the good and bad effects of that choice" [S: May].
- §76: "they are not faithful to the Church's teaching" [S: May].
- §78: "The morality of the human act depends primarily and fundamentally on the 'object' rationally chosen by the deliberate will" [V, peped.org extract; vatican.va not reached].
- §78: "the perspective of the acting person"; the object is "the proximate end of a deliberate decision" [V, same].
- §78: "a freely chosen kind of behavior"; "one cannot mean a process or an event of the merely physical order" [S: May].
- §80: such acts "radically contradict the good of the person made in his image" [S: May].
- §§79, 81–83, and the Latin throughout, are [M]. This includes "intrinsically evil", "always and per se", and "circumstances or intentions can never transform".

**Holds (formal, will)? No, as a reading of their claim.** A node `proportionalist` (Knauer, Schüller, Fuchs, McCormick):
- Parent: `thomist`. Edge: Knauer reads II-II q. 64 a. 7 [V]; solid as a reading.
- Own exclusion: `form_of_act`. Warrant: Knauer §IV, the commensurate reason "alone determines the finis operis" [V].
- Status: McCormick denies the description [S]. The trace cannot show a cause re-defined, only a cause lost (the lying record's strain 7). So both readings run: `proportionalist` excludes the part, and `proportionalist_own` holds it and types their arguments (formal, will) with (final, human), the premoral goods.
- It can replace the contraception record's `revisionist` (Fuchs, Janssens). With the exclusion, that horizon no longer equals `scotus_dpp`'s, which answers that record's strain 2 in part.

*Veritatis splendor* is a source (tier 3), not a horizon. Its §§78–80 arguments are typed (formal, will), and §80's "made in his image" (final, god_ethics).

**Tongue.**
- Thomas's "out of proportion to the end" (q. 64 a. 7) [V; Latin "proportionatus fini" M] measures the act against its end. Knauer's "commensurate reason" measures the reason against the evils. One word-family covers two ratios.
- Whether that is equivocation (May; Finnis [M]) or analogy (Knauer) is a claim, with its claimant.
- The French "raison proportionnée" of 1965 is [M].

## Jeremy Bentham

- "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure" (IPML I.1) [V, laits.utexas.edu].

- "ought, and right and wrong, and others of that stamp, have a meaning: when otherwise, they have none" (I.10) [V].
- "The general tendency of an act is more or less pernicious, according to the sum total of its consequences" (VII.2) [V, econlib].

- "there is no such thing as any sort of motive that is in itself a bad one" (X.2 ¶10) [V]; "If they are good or bad, it is only on account of their effects" (¶12) [V].

**Consequences for the design and the predictions.**

1. **One part, two contrary forms.**
   - Thomas's form is the object, "quodammodo". Kant's is the universality of the maxim, and he defines the matter as "the object of the will" [V], which no material principle can ground [V].
   - With one part, a Kant horizon follows VS 80's "on account of their very object", and a Thomist horizon follows Kant's 1797 argument from "the formal duty" (l_ka1). Kant would refuse the first, as the lying record says he would refuse the (final, human) reading.
   - The alternative is two parts: `object_as_form` (formal, will), and a form of the law in a domain for practical reason, as the lying record's strain 3 already proposed. Kant would exclude the first and hold the second.
2. **The part must name the chosen act.** Otherwise an ethics of intention (Abelard; Ockham on Freddoso's reading) holds it through the interior act's object. The definition above follows VS 78: the object "rationally chosen by the deliberate will".
3. **The part and `ends_desired` touch.** Thomas makes the end the formal part of the whole act (q. 18 a. 6) [V]. VS 78 calls the object "the proximate end". So hv14 and ccc2370, typed (formal, will) or (final, will) in the contraception record, still need their alternatives. The line between the two parts is proximate end against further end (q. 1 a. 3 ad 3; q. 18 a. 6) [V].
4. **Adams's conversion becomes native at Kant.** `conversion_native` fires where (final, will) is held and no final cause in nature, God or human nature is. Kant's node meets that condition, but he does not make ends forces: the end in itself is "not as an end to be effected" [V].
   - The rule should add "and no (formal, will)". Then `hobbes`, `spinoza`, their twins and `bentham` stay native and `kant` does not.
   - CV5's share goes from 1 in 32 to 1 in 64 of the 512 horizons. Without the change it stays 1 in 32, with `kant` and `bentham` added to the native list.
5. **Redefinition is invisible to a trace.** The proportionalists keep the word "object" (finis operis) and re-measure it. Exclusion is the nearest encoding; `proportionalist_own` is its rival.
6. **The new clashes and the contested edges.** The clashes at `hobbes` and `locke` on the part rest on `ockham`* and `hobbes`*. In EX1 both become own exclusions, with the same texts. The clash at `hobbes_wl` stands without the contested edge, since `descartes_will` excludes the part too.

### The thesis: enchantment, not disenchantment

Quotations from the book are kept to the load-bearing few; the rest is paraphrase with its source.

- **Against Weber.** Capitalism has been a regime of enchantment, not an agent of disenchantment (p. 4) [V, WT, paraphrase; page S: Nichols]. "The world does not need to be re-enchanted, because it was never disenchanted in the first place" [V, WT]. He reads Weber as making capitalism, more than science, the cause of the eclipse of the sacred [V, WT, paraphrase].
- **Misenchantment.** Capitalism is "a *mis*enchantment, a parody or perversion of our longing for a sacramental way of being in the world" (p. 5) [V, WT; page S: Smaje, Nichols].
- **A religion.** He takes up Benjamin's claim that capitalism is a religion, a cult with an ontology of its own [V, WT, paraphrase]. The 2015 essay: "a metamorphosis of the sacred in the raiment of secularity" [V, HR 2015].
- **Its sacramentals and its heaven.** Its sacramentals are fetishized commodities and technologies, the material culture of production and consumption (p. 5); its beatific vision is the global rule of capital [V, WT, paraphrase; page S: Nichols]. Consumer culture is "a counterfeit beatific vision" (p. 227, ch. 10) [S: Smaje].
- **A love story.** Capitalism is a misshapen and predatory love of the world, a desire for communion gone wrong; he takes from Eric Gregory that love is the key to history [V, WT, paraphrase]. So the thesis is Augustinian: modernity is a disordered love, not an absence of love.

### Money

- **"Pecuniary metaphysics."** The brief's phrase is not found in the prologue excerpt [V, by absence in WT]. What WT gives: "capitalism is hardly post-metaphysical: its metaphysics is money" [V, WT], money being the criterion of reality, meaning and identity; he also speaks of capitalist metaphysics and of the meretricious ontology of capital [V, WT, paraphrase]. The 2015 essay credits Marx with seeing money as the ground of a uniquely pecuniary way of being [V, HR 2015, paraphrase]; the essay of 2020 is described by its journal as on "the pecuniary theology of neoliberalism" [V, CLJ abstract]. So "pecuniary metaphysics" is a fair label, perhaps a phrase of the body, but not verified as his.
- **"Pecuniary transubstantiation."** Verified, with its subject: Romanticism "has always rejected capitalism's ontology of pecuniary transubstantiation" [V, WT]. The phrase names what capitalism does and what Romanticism refuses. The 2015 essay, after Terrence Tilley: "Commodities are transubstantiated into sacraments … in a world empty of the presence of God" [V, HR 2015; Tilley's own text not fetched].
- **The throne.** "Under capitalism, money occupies the ontological throne from which God has been evicted" [V, WT; also Goodreads, and Russello in *New Rambler*]. It follows his use of Marx's 1844 "Power of Money", where money nullifies even ugliness [V, WT, paraphrase]. Money is capitalism's animating spirit [V, WT, paraphrase].
- **Mammonism.** "Mammonism, the attribution of ontological power to money and of existential sublimity to its possessors" (p. 5) [S: Nichols; the first clause V, WT]. The Gospel of Mammonism licenses counterfeit promissory notes, and the love of money misdirects the sacramental desire for God's presence [V, WT, paraphrase]. He reads Carlyle's Gospel of Mammonism as the good news that money bestows miraculous powers [V, HR 2015, paraphrase]. The promissory note is his figure for money's false promise; whether he means it of credit money as such is not shown [inference].
- **The Eucharistic token.** Of the Puritans: "The Protestant ethic was a gospel of work, with riches as the new Eucharistic tokens of communion with divinity" (p. 37, ch. 1) [S: Nichols]. In 2020, of neoliberalism: "money is the sacramental token, the mana, the elan vital" [V, CLJ]. This is the strongest single warrant for a counterfeit sacrament (below, the trace mode): wealth is put where the Eucharist was, as the sign of communion.
- **The market as divinity.** "the Market becomes the latest modern surrogate for traditional divinity" [V, CLJ]; Hayek and Mises gave money and the market an ontological status as foundational as the God of the Thomists [V, CLJ, paraphrase]. By the postwar years, on his account, God and Mammon had merged (p. 509) [S: Nichols, paraphrase].
- **Grace refused.** On the neoliberal (Randian) view he describes, "To give anyone something for nothing—something called 'grace,' in classical theology—was an act of malice inflicted on the meritorious" [V, CLJ]. It is the warrant closest to A10 (below).
- **Debt, credit and usury.** In the fetched texts, only personal debt taken on against stagnant wages and capital's turn from production to finance [V, CLJ, paraphrase]. Usury appears only in a reviewer's summary of the medieval chapter: believers came to "abandon the Church's prescriptions against usury" [V, Williamson, CWR, his paraphrase]. No fetched text shows McCarraher's own treatment of usury, the just price, or Thomas on money. That ch. 1 treats the medieval "sacramental economy" is [V, its title].

### The medieval baseline

- Ch. 1's subject is "The Medieval Sacramental Economy" [S: WT]. McCarraher reads a barely repressed desire for communism as the political unconscious of medieval Christendom (p. 26) [S: Nichols, who calls it a stretch]. The early Christian communities shared goods, and the commitment "became attenuated" as they spread (Williamson's paraphrase) [V, CWR].
- **Bearing on Graeber.** McCarraher's medieval "communism" is the Christian form of Graeber's baseline communism, and his Middle Ages are Graeber's age of "the return to virtual credit-money" (G2009) [V in `graeber-debt.md`], seen from the sacrament. This is a reading; neither text is shown to cite the other on the point.

### The sacramental imagination and its sources

- **What it is.** "a conviction that the material of ordinary life can mediate the supernatural" [V, HR 2015]; in the book, the earth as a sacramental place that mediates God's presence and power (p. 11) [V, WT, paraphrase; page S: Finkel]. Hopkins's "The world is charged with the grandeur of God" stands at the head [V, WT], and Part One's title is from "God's Grandeur" [inference from the title].
- **Romanticism as the alternative.** Romanticism has been an alternative modernity [V, WT, paraphrase], a critique of the Enlightenment's collusion with bourgeois moralism [V, WT, paraphrase], more resilient and humane than Marxism, progressivism or social democracy (pp. 16–17) [S: Smaje, paraphrase].
- **The roll.** In WT: Carlyle, Ruskin, Morris (*News from Nowhere*), Hopkins, Benjamin, Merton, and from John Muir, William James, Vida Dutton Scudder and Dorothy Day to Lewis Mumford [V, WT]. Ruskin's "there is no wealth but life" [V, HR 2015]. For Ruskin, property private, non-accumulative and convivial (p. 88, ch. 3) [S: Smaje, paraphrase]. The Arts and Crafts binding of necessity and freedom, labour and beauty (p. 95) [S: Nichols, paraphrase].
- **The Catholic and Anglo-Catholic critics.** Dorothy Day is named [V, WT; HNN]. Parramore mentions "Anglo-Catholic sacramentalism" [V, INET, her phrasing]. The distributists are thin: Nichols reports that Chesterton is mentioned twice and distributism given one sentence [S: Nichols]. Chesterton's 1920 remark that American capitalism is a "religion" is reported by Williamson [V, CWR]. Belloc, Gill, Maurin and Penty are not found in any fetched text [V, by absence in WT]. So the brief's expectation of a Catholic distributist core is not borne out: the sources are Romantic and Anglo-American, and the Catholic ones are Day, Merton and the Franciscan way.
- **The Franciscan way.** Franciscan poverty as a path of dispossession rooted in a premodern ontology of love (p. 259, Part Four) [S: Smaje, paraphrase].
- **Aquinas.** Not named in any fetched text. The 2020 essay names "the God of Thomistic scholasticism" as the measure of the market's ontological claim [V, CLJ]. Whether the book cites Thomas on avarice, usury or money is not shown.

### Henry Adams

- **Where.** Part Three's opening (pp. 177–182), not ch. 15. The dynamo of "The Dynamo and the Virgin" (*Education* XXV) is read there: industrial technology as the liturgy of corporate society (p. 178, paraphrase); the sorcerer overwhelmed by his sorcery (p. 178, paraphrase); the dynamo, "a graven image of capitalist modernity" (p. 179) [S: Nichols]. Nichols has the chapter of the *Education* chronicle the Chicago Exposition of 1893 [S: Nichols], which would be ch. XXII ("Chicago (1893)") [M]. The dynamo and the Virgin are ch. XXV, Paris 1900 [V in `modernity-sources.md`].
- **Adams on love and power.** Through Williamson: "Adams doubted that the popular movements of his time could bridge the chasm between power and love"; "Love and power could not be fully reconciled" [V, CWR; Williamson's sentence reporting McCarraher, with a quoted "grotesque alliance"]. Which chapter: likely Part Four, on the cooperative commonwealth [inference].
- **What it gives the study.** McCarraher reads Adams's dynamo as an idol ("graven image") and as a liturgy, not as a force that has replaced an end. Adams himself measured Virgin and dynamo as one "interchangeable force on man" (*Education* XXV) [V in `modernity-sources.md`]. The two readings differ, and both are useful (below, the trace mode): Adams's is a *conversion* (the end measured as force, the study's refused `measures_as`), McCarraher's a *substitution* (the force worshipped in the end's place).

### The proposal: a realized eschatology

- "A realized eschatology: if you will, the future in the present tense" [V, WT, near the end of the prologue]. It follows the claim that any new radicalism must begin from faith in the fundamental joy of being [V, WT, paraphrase].
- The programme, in paraphrase: a Romantic left to wake the country from the American Dream; communists who appeal to the medieval commons rather than to Marx's factory; better work and less of it [V, WT, paraphrase].
- The epilogue (pp. 663–680) calls for a Romantic and sacramental radicalism (p. 674) [S: Finkel, paraphrase], a rapturous ontological wonder (p. 675) [S: Waterfield, paraphrase], and reads Occupy as having revived a languishing hope (p. 673) [S: Waterfield].
- **Graeber.** Named once in WT, in the Occupy passage, quoting Graeber on Occupy as the opening of negotiations over the end of the American empire [V, WT, paraphrase]. No fetched text shows McCarraher using *Debt*, its three moral logics, or the jubilee.
- The goods he sets against capital are the unprofitable ones: love, beauty, contemplation [V, WT, paraphrase]. The unprofitable is the key word for the Grammar (A10, below).

## Avarice and simony

Thomas is quoted from newadvent.org (Dominican Fathers) [V] and, where reached, the Latin from corpusthomisticum.org [V]. Scripture is quoted from newadvent.org's Bible pages, which set the Vulgate and the Greek beside the Knox English [V].

### Avarice: the infinite appetite and the false sufficiency

- **Two riches.** "Naturales quidem divitiae sunt, quibus homini subvenitur ad defectus naturales tollendos"; "Divitiae autem artificiales sunt, quibus secundum se natura non iuvatur, ut denarii" (I-II q. 2 a. 1 co.) [V, Latin]. Artificial wealth "is invented by the art of man" [V, English]. This is the record's domain `art` (`graeber-debt.md`): money's end is (final, art), and the article's argument is that a thing of art cannot be the last end.
- **Ad 3: the bad infinite.** "appetitus divitiarum artificialium est infinitus, quia deservit concupiscentiae inordinatae" [V, Latin]. It is "infinite otherwise than the desire for the sovereign good": "summum bonum quanto perfectius possidetur, tanto ipsummet magis amatur" [V]. Of wealth, "when we already possess them, we despise them, and seek others", for "we realize more their insufficiency when we possess them" [V, English]. So there are two infinities of desire. One grows by possession: the more the good is had, the more it is loved (Sir 24:29, "They that eat me shall yet hunger"). The other grows by want: what is had is despised, and more is sought (Jn 4:13). The second is the first's counterfeit: an appetite with the form of the desire for God, laid on a finite object.
- **The definition and its measure.** Covetousness is "immoderate love of possessing"; riches are sought "according to a certain measure", "in so far as they are necessary for him to live in keeping with his condition of life" (II-II q. 118 a. 1 co.) [V]. The vice is a measure exceeded, and money is itself a measure (*mensura*, record). Avarice is the measure without a measure.
- **Two senses, one external and one interior.** A man may exceed "so as to regard immediately the acquisition and keeping of such things", and then "it is a sin directly against one's neighbor", since one man cannot abound without another lacking, for "temporal goods cannot be possessed by many at the same time". Or he may exceed "in the internal affection which a man has for riches", and then "a man sins against himself"; "As a consequence … it is a sin against God" (a. 1 ad 2) [V]. Thomas draws rule 3's line himself: the first sense is a structure among persons and goods, the second an interior state. The checker may type the first and never the second.
- **Rivalrous goods.** "temporal goods cannot be possessed by many at the same time" (a. 1 ad 2) [V] is A8's premise in Thomas's words: a finite good is rivalrous. With I-II q. 2 a. 1 ad 3 it gives A8 both halves, since the sovereign good is possessed by many without loss.
- **Mortal and venial.** Mortal when the love of riches is "so great as to be preferred to charity"; otherwise "a venial sin" (a. 4) [V]. That is a comparison of loves, interior: not typable.
- **A capital vice: the promise of sufficiency.** A capital vice gives rise to others "under the aspect of end". The most desirable end is happiness, and "one of the conditions of happiness is that it be self-sufficing". "Now riches give great promise of self-sufficiency, as Boethius says"; we "use money in token of taking possession of something" (*Ethic.* V.5); "All things obey money" (Eccl 10:19) (a. 7 co.) [V]. This is the article that carries the apex: money is the capital vice's object because it bears, as a promise, a condition proper to the last end.
- **Not idolatry, but like it.** Eph 5:5 calls the covetous man an idolater: "πλεονέκτης, ὅ ἐστιν εἰδωλολάτρης"; "avarus, quod est idolorum servitus" [V, Greek and Vulgate]. Thomas: "Covetousness is compared to idolatry on account of a certain likeness"; "the covetous man, like the idolater, subjects himself to an external creature, though not in the same way"; the idolater by "paying it Divine honor", the covetous man "by desiring it immoderately for use, not for worship" (a. 5 ad 4) [V]. This bears on McCarraher (below, strain): his "enchantment" reads avarice as worship; Thomas keeps it a likeness.
- **The daughters.** "treachery, fraud, falsehood, perjury, restlessness, violence, and insensibility to mercy" (a. 8) [V, the article's heading]. "Insensibility to mercy" is excess "in retaining", when the heart "is not softened by mercy to assist the needy with his riches"; "restlessness", from "excessive anxiety and care", for "a covetous man shall not be satisfied with money" (Eccl 5:9) [V]. Fraud, perjury and violence are acts against others, typable as acts; restlessness and insensibility are interior, not typable.
- **Trade for gain (q. 77 a. 4), as context.** Exchange for need "is commendable because it supplies a natural need"; exchange for profit serves a craving that "knows no limit and tends to infinity", and has "a certain debasement attaching thereto", since it does not of itself aim at a virtuous or necessary end. It becomes lawful when gain is "directed to some necessary or even virtuous end" [V, as returned]. The just price: "equality of thing and thing" (a. 1) [V]. So the structure Thomas refuses is gain held as a terminal end, not gain as such. Q. 78 (usury) is in the usury record [V there].
- **1 Tim 6:10.** "ῥίζα γὰρ πάντων τῶν κακῶν ἐστιν ἡ φιλαργυρία"; the Vulgate "Radix enim omnium malorum est cupiditas" [V]. The Latin says *cupiditas*, not *avaritia*: the Greek's love of silver is widened to desire. A tongue note for TTC-LINGUAE: an argument resting on "the love of money" rests on the Greek. 6:18 sets against it "κοινωνικούς", "communicare" [V], the sharing that is Graeber's baseline communism in Paul's word.

### Mammon, the rival master

- **Mt 6:24.** "Οὐδεὶς δύναται δυσὶ κυρίοις δουλεύειν"; "οὐ δύνασθε θεῷ δουλεύειν καὶ μαμωνᾷ"; "Non potestis Deo servire et mammonæ" [V]. The Lucan doublet, Lk 16:13, has the same last clause [V]. The verb is a slave's service (*douleuein*), and the rivalry is of masters: one is loved, the other hated (6:24a) [V].
- **Lk 16:9, 11.** "facite vobis amicos de mammona iniquitatis"; "ἐκ τοῦ μαμωνᾶ τῆς ἀδικίας"; "in iniquo mammona fideles non fuistis" [V]. Mammon is unjust, and still to be used: a good of art with a proper use, ordered to friendship and the "true" riches (Knox: "who will put the true riches in your keeping?") [V]. So the Gospel itself refuses to make money evil by nature. The evil is its taking the master's place.
- **The 2000 campaign's phrase.** Archbishop Welby called the Jubilee 2000 debt campaign "perhaps the churches' finest hour in dethroning Mammon" [S: Wikipedia, "Jubilee 2000"].

### Simony: money in the order of grace

- **Acts 8:18–20.** Simon offered money for "hanc potestatem", "τὴν ἐξουσίαν ταύτην"; Peter: "τὴν δωρεὰν τοῦ θεοῦ ἐνόμισας διὰ χρημάτων κτᾶσθαι", "donum Dei existimasti pecunia possideri" [V]. The sin is a judgement about a kind: he thought the *gift* (*dōrea*) acquirable by money.
- **Mt 10:8.** "δωρεὰν ἐλάβετε, δωρεὰν δότε"; "gratis accepistis, gratis date" [V]. *Dōrean* is the adverb of *dōrea*: the gift's mode is part of what it is.
- **Thomas, II-II q. 100 a. 1.** Simony is "an express will to buy or sell something spiritual or connected with a spiritual thing" [V, Dominican Fathers]. (The brief's "deliberate" and "annexed" are another translation's; the Latin *studiosa voluntas* is [M].) Three reasons, each a different structure:
  1. **No price measures it.** "a spiritual thing cannot be appraised at any earthly price" [V].
  2. **The minister is not owner.** "a thing cannot be due matter for sale if the vendor is not the owner thereof"; "ecclesiastical superiors are not owners, but dispensers of spiritual things" [V].
  3. **The source is a gift.** "sale is opposed to the source of spiritual things, since they flow from the gratuitous will of God" [V]. Buying or selling them is therefore irreverence, a sin of irreligion [V, as returned].
- **Sacraments and support.** The sacraments are "of all things most spiritual", and their grace is "essentially incompatible with a non-gratuitous giving" (a. 2) [V]. What ministers receive is "not as a price of goods, but as a payment for their need"; the priest "receives money, not as the price for consecrating the Eucharist" (a. 2 co., ad 2) [V]. In spiritual actions, temporal things are given "not as a price but as a means of livelihood" (a. 3) [V]. The simoniac "shall forfeit the dignity or cure that he has acquired with his money" (a. 6, citing the canons) [V].
- **The distinction the law keeps.** Price (*pretium*) against support (*sustentatio*, *stips*, *oblatio*). One payment can be either; the classification decides. That is rule 1's `classification/3`: theology fixes that grace has no price, and the law classifies the payment.

## Sin as debt

### The texts

- **Mt 6:12.** "ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, ὡς καὶ ἡμεῖς ἀφήκαμεν τοῖς ὀφειλέταις ἡμῶν"; "dimitte nobis debita nostra, sicut et nos dimittimus debitoribus nostris" [V]. The Knox English has "trespasses" [V]: the debt is gone from the English most Catholics pray.
- **Lk 11:4.** "ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν, καὶ γὰρ αὐτοὶ ἀφίομεν παντὶ ὀφείλοντι ἡμῖν"; "dimitte nobis peccata nostra, siquidem et ipsi dimittimus omni debenti nobis" [V]. Luke has "sins" in the first clause and "every one owing us" in the second: the two words in one petition, which is the equation's warrant in the Gospels.
- **Mt 18:23–35.** The servant "ὀφειλέτης μυρίων ταλάντων", "qui debebat ei decem millia talenta"; "μὴ ἔχοντος δὲ αὐτοῦ ἀποδοῦναι"; the lord "τὸ δάνειον ἀφῆκεν αὐτῷ", "debitum dimisit ei"; "πᾶσαν τὴν ὀφειλὴν ἐκείνην ἀφῆκά σοι" (vv. 24, 25, 27, 32) [V]. Ten thousand talents is a debt that cannot be paid; it is released, not settled.
- **Col 2:14.** "ἐξαλείψας τὸ καθ' ἡμῶν χειρόγραφον τοῖς δόγμασιν"; "delens quod adversus nos erat chirographum decreti" [V]. A *cheirographon* is a handwritten note of debt [M for the lexicon]; the newadvent note reads it of the Mosaic law [V, Knox note]. The bond is erased, not paid.
- **Prov 19:17.** "He who is kind to the poor lends to the Lord and will be repaid in full" [S: Neff's quotation, *Christianity Today*]. Not fetched in Hebrew or Latin.

### Anderson, *Sin: A History* (Yale 2009)

Not opened. Through four reviews:

- **The shift.** "Sin as a burden comprises the most common metaphor for sin in the First Temple period" (p. 17); later sin is a "bond of indebtedness deducted from one's balance sheet" (p. 29) [S: Kiel, *JHS* 11 (2011)]. - **Its cause.** "the rise of Aramaic as the official language of law and commerce" (Gula's report) [V]. - **Isa 40:2 and the bond.** God holds "a bond" against the sinner; Isa 40:2 is the anchor [S: Brueggemann]. רצה comes to mean one "quit of his obligation to repay debt that has accrued through sin" (p. 53) [S: Kiel].

### Anselm and Thomas

- **Anselm, *Cur Deus homo* I.11.** "This is the debt which man and angel owe to God, and no one who pays this debt commits sin"; "so long as he does not restore what he has taken away, he remains in fault" [S: Wikipedia, "Satisfaction theory of atonement", which gives I.11 with I.7 for the first]. - **Thomas, I-II q. 87.** "the debt of punishment is considered to be directly the effect of sin" (a. 1 ad 2); when the act has ceased "there still remains the debt of punishment" (a. 6) [V]. - **III q. 48 a. 4.** Man was held "as to the debt of punishment, to the payment of which man was held fast by God's justice"; the Passion "was as a price at the cost of which we were freed from both obligations"; "Christ made satisfaction, not by giving money or anything of the sort"; "the price had to be paid not to the devil, but to God" (ad 3) [V]. ### What this gives the question

- The equation sin = debt is real in the texts (Lk 11:4's two words in one petition; Mt 18; Col 2:14) and dated (Anderson's Second Temple shift). It is an analogy with a limit the texts themselves mark: the debt to God is unpayable (ten thousand talents), and it ends by release (*aphesis*, *dimittere*), erasure (*exaleipsas*), or a price "not by giving money" (III q. 48 a. 4).
- So sin as debt does not put grace into exchange's grammar if the limit is kept: the debt is entered by analogy, with God's side not discharged by equivalence. Brueggemann's worry is what follows if the analogy is read univocally. ## The jubilee

### The texts

- **Lev 25:10.** "vocabis remissionem cunctis habitatoribus terræ tuæ: ipse est enim jubilæus"; LXX "διαβοήσετε ἄφεσιν ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς" [V]. Hebrew *dĕrôr*, cognate with Akkadian *andurārum* (Hudson) [V in `graeber-debt.md`, paraphrase as returned]. Wikipedia notes *dĕrôr* for the release of slaves in Jeremiah and "a year of liberty (שנת דרור)" in Ezekiel [S: Wikipedia, "Jubilee (biblical)"].
- **Lev 25:23.** "Terra quoque non vendetur in perpetuum, quia mea est, et vos advenæ et coloni mei estis"; "ἐμὴ γάρ ἐστιν ἡ γῆ" [V]. The jubilee rests on God's ownership: no creature holds the land in perpetuity.
- **Lev 25:35–37.** For the brother who has become poor: "ne accipias usuras ab eo, nec amplius quam dedisti"; "Pecuniam tuam non dabis ei ad usuram"; LXX "τόκον", "ἐπὶ τόκῳ" [V]. The usury ban sits inside the jubilee chapter.
- **Deut 15.** "Septimo anno facies remissionem"; "quia annus remissionis est Domini"; LXX "ἑπτὰ ἐτῶν ποιήσεις ἄφεσιν", "ἐπικέκληται ἄφεσις κυρίῳ" (vv. 1–2) [V]. "omnino indigens et mendicus non erit inter vos" (v. 4) and "Non deerunt pauperes in terra habitationis tuæ" (v. 11) [V]: the release is commanded both because there should be no poor and because there will always be.
- **Isa 61:1–2 and Lk 4:18–19.** Isa 61:1 LXX "κηρύξαι αἰχμαλώτοις ἄφεσιν", Latin "prædicarem captivis indulgentiam"; 61:2 "ἐνιαυτὸν κυρίου δεκτὸν", "annum placabilem Domino" [V]. Lk 4:18 "κηρύξαι αἰχμαλώτοις ἄφεσιν" and "ἀποστεῖλαι τεθραυσμένους ἐν ἀφέσει"; 4:19 "κηρύξαι ἐνιαυτὸν κυρίου δεκτόν", "prædicare annum Domini acceptum" [V].
- **One word.** The Greek uses *aphesis* for the jubilee (Lev 25:10), the seventh-year release of debts (Deut 15:1–2), the release of captives (Isa 61:1; Lk 4:18), and, as *aphesis hamartiōn*, the forgiveness of sins elsewhere [M for the last]; the verb *aphiēmi* forgives debts in Mt 6:12 and 18:27, 32 [V]. The Latin splits it: *remissio*, *indulgentia*, *dimittere* [V]. A tongue note for TTC-LINGUAE: the jubilee and forgiveness are one act in Greek and three words in Latin. In English "release", "liberty", "forgiveness" and "remission" divide it again.
- **Was it kept?** Wikipedia: "the practices prescribed for the Jubilee year have not been observed for many centuries", and sources disagree on "whether it was ever put into effect on a nationwide basis" [S: Wikipedia]. For the checker that is a fact about history; the law's form is not changed by it.

### Hudson's clean slates, as Graeber uses them

- Graeber names "economist Michael Hudson" as his source on Mesopotamia (G2009) [V in `graeber-debt.md`]; he lists "Mesopotamian clean slates, Biblical Jubilees, Medieval laws against usury" together (GP2011) [V there].
- Hudson: the rulers "were not seeking a debt-free utopia but coped pragmatically with the most adverse consequences of rural debt"; "Usury was not banned, as it would be in Judaism's Exodus Code" (2024) [V there]. Wikipedia reports Hudson's view that the Jubilee and sabbatical laws "addressed the same problems" as the Babylonian decrees, codified as law [S: Wikipedia, "Jubilee (biblical)"]. Hudson's ... *and forgive them their debts* (2018) is [M].
- **The difference that matters for the Grammar.** The Mesopotamian release is a king's act, *ad hoc*, from above: D. The Levitical release is a law of the calendar, on God's title to the land (25:23), owed by all to all: not a sovereign's grace but the order restored. Atik's objection stands for both: "A Jubilee is, at best, an accommodation with debt — it is not the destruction of debt" [V in `graeber-debt.md`].

- **Tertio millennio adveniente 51 (1994).** Through the Jubilee 2000 magazine (vatican.va, 1 Dec. 1997) [S]: the Pope proposed "the Jubilee as an appropriate time to give thought" to "reducing substantially, if not cancelling outright, the international debt". The same page: "The jubilee year was meant to restore equality among all the children of Israel"; "Justice, according to the Law of Israel, consisted above all in the protection of the weak" [S; that these are TMA 13's own words is M]. The vatican.va text of TMA itself was not reached.
- **Spes non confundit 16 (9 May 2024).** It asks "more affluent nations" to "forgive the debts of countries that will never be able to repay them" [S: Giraud, Nubukpo and Rodarie, *La Civiltà Cattolica*, 12 Mar. 2025, who give the paragraph as 16]. The date of the bull is [S: Wikipedia]. That SNC 16 adds "More than a question of generosity, this is a matter of justice" and names the "ecological debt" is [M]; the vatican.va text was not reached. So the brief's "if it says so" is answered: it does ask for the forgiveness of debts, at no. 16 [S].
- **Typing.** Both appeals are tier-3 or lower acts by form (rule 14) and argue from Lev 25 and justice to the weak. In the study's sorts they argue from (final, god_ethics) (the land is the Lord's) and (final, human) (the protection of the weak); neither argues from the nature of money. That fits Graeber's own case against debt (final, human), and not Thomas's against usury (final, art) (`graeber-debt.md`).

```clingo
% Enter mammon as inanimate(mammon): creature/1, so ind_sort(mammon,cr) and finite(mammon).
violation(self_given_to_thing(X,Y)) :- uses(icon), total(X,Y), created_person(X), not person(Y).
crosses(last_end,X)            :- last_end(_,X), ind_sort(X,cr).
% R: release is not payment.
violation(release_as_payment(A)) :- releases(A,X,Y), settles(A,X,Y).
```

### Where it holds and where it strains

- **Holds best.** Three pieces type with rules already in the theory: Mt 6:24 by A8, a debt pledged for another by A5's `consent_supplied`, and simony's first reason by the kernel's `sort_clash` (`cand_grace_stuff`, below). They type a structure among persons and goods, and stay on the external side of Thomas's line in q. 118 a. 1 ad 2.
- **Strains.** The Grammar has one closing, N's, and refuses every closing without a bond. Graeber's exchange is a second closing, just in itself, and the theory has no place for it except refusal. Adding E is the price of the mapping: without it, the Grammar would refuse the just price.
- **A11 is the wrong rule for Mammon.** The brief's mapping (Mammon ~ `cand_apex`) puts the error on the wrong side of the line. The kernel already refuses a creature's predicate on God (`crosses(finite,X)`); Mammon is a divine predicate (last end, sufficiency) on a creature. The fix is a crossing, not a new axiom.
- **"Bond" is equivocal.** English *bond* names the marriage *vinculum* and the debt instrument; Latin *nexum* was Roman debt-bondage [M]. A6's `proposed_bond` must not be used for debt. A tongue note for TTC-LINGUAE.

## A sin type, structurally

Rule 3: the checker never computes imputability, intention or interior state. Thomas draws the line in the article that defines the vice: avarice "so as to regard immediately the acquisition and keeping of such things" is "a sin directly against one's neighbor"; avarice "in the internal affection" is a sin "against himself" (II-II q. 118 a. 1 ad 2) [V]. The first is a structure, the second a state. Mortal or venial turns on whether riches are "preferred to charity" (a. 4) [V]: a comparison of loves, never computed.

The Magisterium names the same kind: "structures of sin", developed in *Sollicitudo rei socialis* 36 from *Reconciliatio et paenitentia* (1984) [S: Wikipedia, "Sollicitudo rei socialis"]; the Catechism's "social situations or institutions that are contrary to the divine law" [S: same, citing the CCC]. That RP 16 calls social sin sin by analogy, and that SRS 37 names "the all-consuming desire for profit" and "the thirst for power", is [M]. If it holds, the types below are sin only by analogy, which is what rule 3 needs: a structure of sin is never a person's sin.

## Misenchantment as a trace mode

### What the study has

A horizon is the set of parts it holds; a trace is a part not held, and the reports say what is then held without reason (`without_reason`, `sole_reason`, `contrary_reasoned`). One part is added rather than traced: Kant's `form_of_law`. The measure `measures_as(adams_1907, final, efficient)` is refused save where `conversion_native` fires (Hobbes, Spinoza and their twins) (`modernity-preregistration.md`, CV5) [V, read].

### What McCarraher adds

The trace is not empty. "The world does not need to be re-enchanted, because it was never disenchanted in the first place" [V, WT]; capitalism is "a parody or perversion" of the sacramental (p. 5) [V, WT]; "a counterfeit beatific vision" (p. 227) [S: Smaje]. Where Weber sees subtraction, McCarraher sees substitution: something stands in the traced part's place and does its office.

### Adams has both moves in one chapter

- **The conversion.** The Virgin "had drawn man's activities to herself", and "both energies acted as interchangeable force on man" (*Education* XXV) [V in `modernity-sources.md`]: the end's drawing measured as force.
- **The substitution.** The dynamo "became a symbol of infinity"; "Before the end, one began to pray to it" (XXV) [V there]: the force loved as an end. McCarraher reads this second move: the dynamo is "a graven image of capitalist modernity" (p. 179), and industrial technology the liturgy of corporate society (p. 178) [S: Nichols, paraphrase].
- **Interchangeable is money's word.** Money "acting as a measure, makes goods commensurate and equates them" (NE V.5) [V in `graeber-debt.md`]. Adams's "interchangeable force" is commensuration applied to causes: the pecuniary act. So the conversion is what money does to ends, and the prayer is what follows when the measure is left in the end's place. This is a reading; Adams does not name money in the passage fetched.

### Proposal: a substitution report, read through the bridge

Three ways to encode it, in the order I recommend.

1. **A claim of substitution, reported on the bridge (recommended).** Enter `substitutes(R, A, P, Q, Claimant)`: the reading R of argument A, typed by the traced part P, is re-read as resting on Q, on a claimant's word (McCarraher; Carlyle; Benjamin). It is a claim, as Fortin's shorter argument is (`concludes_heard`, TTC-LINGUAE): the checker never decides that money *is* a counterfeit. With a host that holds P and a guest that traces it, the VM reports `counterfeit(Host, Guest, A, P, Q, Claimant)`: in the guest, A's conclusion is carried by R; seen from the host, R stands where P stood. A new report beside the three:
   - `counterfeit_reasoned(Q,V,R)`: V is held in the guest only by a substituted reading.
   - It is native where the conversion is: in a guest whose ends survive only as desire, the substitute is not a counterfeit but the definition. Hobbes: "The value of all things contracted for is measured by the appetite of the contractors" (*Lev.* 15) [V in `graeber-debt.md`]. So "pathological" is a host's judgement on a guest, and the study already computes host against guest. That keeps it out of the horizon's own parts and answers Williamson's objection to a literal "enchantment": the counterfeit is a relation between horizons, not an entity.
2. **A part modernity adds (alternative).** A part `measure_as_end` held only where a runtime adds it, as `form_of_law` is. It fails on sorts: what it holds is (final, art), which the proposed `ends_of_art` already holds as money's *finis operis* (exchange). The counterfeit is the same cause in another office, so a part would duplicate a cause. Keep it only if the record shows a figure who holds the measure as last end in his own words.
3. **The inverse measure (alternative).** Enter `measures_as(mccarraher_2019, efficient, final)` beside Adams's, refused save where native, with a rule of its own (the study refuses only `cause_converted(M, final, efficient)`). It is simple and matches the chapter's two moves, but it puts a historian's thesis in the slot meant for a measure, and money is a measure by art, not an efficient cause. Use it only for Adams's own prayer: `measures_as(adams_1907_prayer, efficient, final)`, warranted by XXV [V there].

### What depends on what

- **The `art` domain.** Without it, the substitute is (final, will), money as desired, and folds into Hobbes's native conversion: the trace mode cannot be told from the trace. With it, the counterfeit is (final, art) in the office of (final, god_ethics). So this proposal depends on the decision on `art` (`graeber-debt.md`, Recommendation).
- **The pairs it would report.** Read from McCarraher's own claims, each to be entered with him as claimant:

| Traced part | Substitute | McCarraher's warrant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ends_by_god_ethics` (final, god_ethics) | money as last end, (final, art) | "money occupies the ontological throne from which God has been evicted" [V, WT]; the Market "the latest modern surrogate for traditional divinity" [V, CLJ] |
| the sacramental (no part yet: TTC-MODERNITY has no supernatural domain, `case-religious-liberty.md`) | wealth as Eucharistic token | p. 37 [S: Nichols]; CLJ 2020 [V] |
| `ends_in_nature`, `forms_in_nature` | the technological sublime, the machine as liturgy | p. 135 [S: Smaje]; p. 178 [S: Nichols] |

- **The sacramental has no part.** The second row needs a part the study does not have. The religious-liberty record found no supernatural domain needed; this record finds one place that would use it. Left open.

### Where the texts read two ways

- **Idolatry or use.** McCarraher reads the love of money as worship, a religion with its idolatry [V, WT, paraphrase]. Thomas keeps it a likeness: "for use, not for worship" (q. 118 a. 5 ad 4) [V]. Both readings should run: `mccarraher_worship` (the substitute takes the last end's honour: T2 and T4) and `thomas_use` (the substitute takes the last end's sufficiency without honour: T2 alone).
- **Subtraction or substitution.** Weber's trace (subtraction) is the study's design; McCarraher's substitution is a reading of the same history. Since the substitution is entered as a claim, both run with no change to the parts.
- **Enchantment literal or analogical.** Williamson objects to the literal use [V]. In the checker the substitute is analogical by construction: a counterfeit is typed by its office, never by the sort it counterfeits.

### What challenges the design

1. **McCarraher is not decisive for the typing.** His book is a cultural history of American economic religion; its theology is in the prologue, ch. 1–3 and the epilogue. It names Graeber once, on Occupy, and the fetched texts show nothing on usury, the just price, credit or the jubilee. What it gives the study is one thesis, substitution, and the counterfeit sacrament. The texts that type the structures are Thomas's (I-II q. 2 a. 1; II-II qq. 77, 100, 118) and the canons.
2. **A11 is not the apex rule.** The brief's `cand_apex` mapping is the converse of Mammon's error (above). The design should use the kernel's crossing instead.
3. **The Grammar refuses exchange.** As it stands, any closing without a bond is refused; the just price would be refused if typed. E is needed before Graeber's three logics can enter.
4. **A10 measures rankings, not prices.** Money's measure on a person needs the rule widened, with the token kept.
5. **Thomas against the enchantment thesis.** q. 118 a. 5 ad 4 denies that avarice is idolatry in kind. A trace mode that types modernity as idolatry rests on McCarraher's claim, not on Thomas, and must be entered as his.

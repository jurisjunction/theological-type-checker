# Peters on signs: canonlaw.info, *In the Light of the Law* and three articles, the evidence record for TTC-LINGUAE

This is a research record for TTC-LINGUAE, compiled on 23 October 2026, beside `linguae-signs.md`. It is an evidence record, so it keeps prose (rule 10). It covers what Dr. Edward N. Peters has published on his own site about deaf Catholics, sign languages and the sacraments. That is: his two ASL essays, his bibliography, his blog, his faculty page, his NCOD committee page, and the PDFs he hosts of his three articles. It records his positions as his claims, and it checks the primary sources he cites as far as they could be reached.

**How sure each item is:**
- **[V]** The wording was checked against a text fetched for this record. For Peters's own pages and PDFs, [V] means his wording was fetched. It does not mean that what he says of a third text is true.
- **[S]** It was checked through a named secondary source that quotes or reports the primary. In this record that source is usually Peters himself.
- **[M]** It comes from memory or a standard citation and was not re-checked.

Quotations are under 25 words. They came through a fetch tool that returns short extracts, so a quotation is exact as returned, not proof-read against print. The three PDFs are OCR scans. The tool says it corrected some OCR slips (e.g. "Gin" for "(in"), so a [V] quotation from a PDF is "as returned". Page numbers are those the tool reported. "Observation" marks a comparison that no fetched source makes. "Proposal" marks this record's design.

Abbreviations: **ASL I** (1997), **ASL II** (2001), **Blog 2008**, **Bib.** (the bibliography), **NCOD page**, **SC 42** (*Studia Canonica* 42, 2008), **JJT 15** (*Josephinum* 15, 2008), **J 73** (*The Jurist* 73, 2013). Each is listed under Pages read.

Peters calls him "the first culturally Deaf man to be ordained to priesthood and immediately assigned to active ministry" (SC 42 n. 7) [V].
   - Peters also reports earlier deaf ordinations under dispensation (Deaf priests, below). So "first" depends on the description.

The US bishops asked in November 1966, and the Holy See approved "in January/February 1967" [S: NCOD page, citing the commission's *Newsletter* 2:11 (Nov. 1966) 58–59 and 3:3 (Mar. 1967) 73].

## His positions

### Signed forms

- **Thesis.** "I believe that the modern praxis Ecclesiae is correct" and that "the ordination of men bereft of speech and the celebration of sacraments solely in sign is valid and licit" (SC 42 p. 336) [V].
- **The question he poses.** In 1997: "whether a celebrant could offer Mass solely in sign language, without using an approved oral language (here, English) at the same time, even inaudibly" (ASL I) [V]. Its scope "addresses issues which are much more theological than linguistic or pastoral" [V].
  - In 2008 the question is whether sacraments "can be celebrated solely in sign language, that is, without any oral pronouncement of the form" (SC 42 n. 5) [V].
- **Signed and spoken together is not in question.** "no one challenges the validity of sacraments celebrated in sign and speech" (SC 42 n. 5) [V]. Any challenge would have to overcome "Pope Paul VI's express approval of the simultaneous use of sign language and oral speech" [V]. That is, he sets a governance act against a doubt of validity (SG5 below).
- **The argument.**
  - "what is crucial is not the language the minister uses, but rather the ability of that language to express or communicate" the form's sense (SC 42 p. 343) [V].
  - "what is required for sacramental form is the direct expression or communication of the form" (p. 343) [V].
  - Thomas, ST I q. 34 a. 1: the interior concept is the word first, the vocal sound secondarily (p. 342 n. 36) [V for Peters's use].
  - Thomas, ST III q. 60 a. 8 ad 2: "Words belong to a sacramental form by reason of the sense signified by them" (p. 342) [V].
  - Sign and writing. "Sign language, in contrast to writing, is not a secondary representation of mental concepts, but rather, an immediate presentation of same." (p. 340 n. 32) [V]. Earlier authors rejected writing as a way to express form, "correctly in my opinion" (D'Annibale) [V].
  - Sensibility. "the form of any sacrament, expressed competently in a sign language, is completely sense-perceptible" (p. 344) [V], and it "accurately conveys to third parties (who know the language) the meaning intended by Christ" [V]. It is "perfectly sensible to anyone who knows sign language" (p. 344) [V]. Observation: his sensibility is indexed to a hearer who knows the language.
  - Regatillo's standard is met where the words "propose to the congregants such a sense by common understanding" (p. 345) [V for Peters].
- **His conditions.**
  - Sign languages are "independent authentic human languages" (p. 340) [V].
  - He excludes "bizarre methods" (p. 344 n. 43) [V]. The tool summarised his examples, not quoting them, as pantomime, Morse-code blinking and lip-syncing.
  - "not every deaf man is necessarily skilled in sign language", so signing ability "should be assessed, with the help of experts if necessary" (p. 343 n. 42) [V].
  - He does not name manually coded English in SC 42 [V, by absence].
- **The authorities against him.**
  - Commentators "held for the use of oral speech in celebrating the sacraments" (p. 335) [V].
  - Regatillo: "The form must be pronounced for validity: a) vocally" (p. 335) [S].
  - Halligan: absolution "to be valid must be oral, even in the greatest necessity" (p. 336 n. 17) [S].
  - Cappello on absolution (p. 335) [S]. Hervé, Noldin–Schmitt–Heinzel and McHugh–Callan are cited as requiring an audible form [S, as summarised].
  - Those authors argued "a divine law obstacle to orders based on mutism", not deafness (p. 334 n. 9) [V].
- **Pius XII (1956) and the Holy Office (1957).** They appear only in n. 41, where Peters reads Pius XII as reiterating "the importance of communicating or expressing the form of a sacrament" (p. 343) [V].
  - He does not read them, as McNamara does, as directed against the merely mental *verbum mentis* [V, by absence].
  - He cites the 1957 response without comment [V].
- **Validity and liceity** are treated together for signed celebration. The one distinction he draws: "Irregularities impact only the liceity, not the validity, of ordination" (p. 332 n. 4) [V].
- **Liturgical translation** (cc. 838, 841, 846 §1) "is beyond the scope of this article" (p. 343 n. 40) [V].
- **Conclusion.** "There is, in brief, no obstacle in divine or ecclesiastical law according to which deafness" or lack of speech are "obstacles to the celebration of sacraments" (p. 345) [V].
  - So Masses and absolutions "celebrated by Deaf clergy without vocalization over the past 40 years were not invalid" (p. 345 n.) [V].
  - The practice "deepens our understanding of the 'expression requirement' of sacramental form" (p. 345 n.) [V].
- **Practice as ground.** "Ecclesiae praxis aliquando docet doctrinam Ecclesiae". "St. Thomas, Regatillo, and Cappello provided the necessary tools" (Comments, 3 Jan 2008) [V].
- **What he leaves open.** "practical pastoral or liturgical questions about proclaiming sacramental form in sign languages" remain. They "are better addressed when specific attention can be accorded them" (SC 42 p. 344 n. 42) [V].
  - He does not say the doctrinal question is open, or that it awaits the Holy See [V, by absence]. He cites no act of the Holy See on a form signed alone [V, by absence].

### Consent

- **His site has no reading of c. 1104 §2.** No page read treats *verba* against *signa aequipollentia*, c. 1106, or whether an assistant who does not know ASL can receive consent under c. 1108 §2 [V, by absence in every page listed above; the Directory has no entry under 1104 or 1106].
- **One statement.** A commenter asked whether deaf, mute spouses exchanging signs effect a sacrament, so that "a sacrament has been effected without using sounds or vocalizations". Peters: "Answer, Yes." (Comments, 3 Jan 2008) [V]. This is a claim of validity only.
- **Deaf clergy as assistants.** Deaf clergy "witnessed scores of weddings" (SC 42 n. 6) [V].
- **Presence.** J 73 uses c. 1104 §1 (proxy) to argue that physical presence "is certainly not required for valid and licit celebration of matrimony" (p. 531) [V]. He uses it for moral presence in confession, not for consent in sign.
- **Interpreters.** ASL II, on a liturgy interpreter: "this legitimate concern has to do with the fidelity of the interpreter, not the nature of sign language" [V].
- **Listed, not read.** G. Richards, "Deaf people, the pre-nuptial inquiry, and annulments", *CLS GB&I Newsletter* 113 (1998) 58–63 [S: Bib.; SC 42 n. 1].

### Confession

- **The penitent may confess without voice.** Peters's English of Cappello: "It does not go to the validity of the sacrament that confession be vocal" (J 73 p. 524) [V for Peters; S for Cappello].
- **Absolution in sign.** "absolution conferred in sign language is, we may say now, a valid and licit means" of expressing the form (p. 526) [V].
  - Cappello's insistence on a voice is "no longer sustainable" (p. 526) [V].
  - In fact, "hearing priests frequently do not 'voice' the form of absolution" when signing (p. 525) [V].
  - He rests this on SC 42: "I have dealt thoroughly with this question" (p. 525) [V].
- **Interpreters are permitted, never required.** "the use of interpreters cannot be required of those wishing to make penance" (p. 517) [V].
  - His authorities: Propaganda Fide, 6 September 1630 (the table below); McManus, "The canon permits, but does not require"; Damizia, "Non é obbligatorio ricorrere all'interprete" [S].
- **Why interpreters and writing fall short.** The problems of written and interpreted confession "are so serious" (p. 519) [V]. "Written confession, however, is onerous" (p. 515) [V].
  - He quotes Dom Augustine: "Confessing through an interpreter is extraordinary" (p. 517) [S].
  - He quotes McAreavey: the confessor is "seriously handicapped in discerning the state of the soul" (p. 518) [S].
  - Integrality is then excused, and the excuse becomes chronic (p. 519; Jone). This is the tool's summary, not his wording.
- **The seal.** Interpreters must grasp "the gravity of the obligation of confidentiality they incur" (p. 518) [V]. He cites cc. 983 §2 and 1388 §2, the numbering before 2021 [V].
- **The 1917 rule.** In his English, c. 903 reads: "Whoever cannot otherwise confess is not prohibited if they want from confessing through an interpreter." (p. 517 n. 9) [V]
- **Video.** Live video gives the mutual moral presence that confession needs (§7; the tool's summary). It "must be 'live' for validity of the celebration" (p. 535 n. 52) [V].
  - It should be authorised by a specific written grant of faculties (p. 536) [V].
  - For hearing penitents the question "need not be settled before recognizing its use by the Deaf" (p. 535) [V]. He leaves it open.

### Deaf priests

- **The 1917 irregularity.** "Several commentators on Pio-Benedictine law listed the deaf as examples of those irregular" under c. 984 2° (JJT 15 p. 427) [V].
  - It was "subject to dispensation by the Holy See" (p. 428) [V]. Petitions usually went to the Congregation of the Sacraments (n. 6, after Martin 1913) [S].
  - Ayrinhac (1928): dispensation was granted in some cases, and refused where the applicant "would have been unable to hear the server at Mass" (n. 6) [S].
- **Earlier cases.**
  - "in the 1920s, an adventitiously deaf man was ordained in England and perhaps one in France" (p. 428) [V].
  - The Holy Office "permit d'ordonner un religieux sourd-muet de naissance", 12 November 1929, after de Clercq; Cance dates the same case 1919 (n. 7) [S].
  - A Brazilian deaf priest celebrated in Detroit in 1953, and a South American was ordained but barred from offering Mass (n. 8) [S, as summarised].
- **The modern pioneers.** Axelrod (1970, Port Elizabeth), Coughlin (1977, "ordained for priestly ministry with the Trinitarians") and Deacon Keil "all achieved ordination under the Pio-Benedictine Code" (JJT 15 pp. 429, 438) [V]. They did not wait for the new Code "to remove the irregularity of physical defect" [V].
  - JJT 15 does not say whether they were dispensed [V, by absence].
- **The 1983 Code.**
  - The first schema (*Schema de Sacramentis*, c. 224 §1) kept a bodily irregularity (n. 43) [S].
  - The coetus suppressed it: "Ratione habita eorum qui handicappati vocantur… supprimitur § 1" (*Communicationes* 10, 196) (n. 44) [S].
  - c. 1041 lists no physical defect (n. 41) [V for Peters; V for the canon in the record].
  - A link between the first ordinations and the change "probably cannot be established" (n. 44) [V].
  - The blog: "restrictions against ordaining the deaf have been eliminated from the revised law" (Blog 2008) [V].
- **Divine law.** Cappello: "Mutus, qui nullo modo loqui valet, non solum est irregularis, sed ipso iure divino a statu clericali arcetur" (SC 42 n. 10) [S]. Wernz–Vidal held such ordinations valid but illicit; Hickey reports unnamed authors who held them invalid (SC 42) [S, as summarised]. Peters rejects every divine-law obstacle (p. 345) [V].
- **Numbers.**
  - "at least a dozen deaf men have already been ordained to priesthood" in the United States since 1977 (Blog 2008) [V].
  - Since 1977, "more than a dozen Deaf men have been ordained to priesthood or permanent diaconate in the United States" (SC 42 n. 7) [V].
  - Observation: the two counts differ in scope.
- **Mass in ASL.**
  - Officials are "quite clear that ASL interpretation of Mass is entirely licit" (ASL I) [V].
  - Peters served on the NCOD Religious Sign Translation Committee (2006–2011, faculty page) [V]. Its project was "to develop an accurate American Sign Language translation of the Mass directly from the Latin editio typica" (NCOD page) [V]. No approval of it is reported [V, by absence].
  - Deaf clergy are "about as close to 'irreplaceable' in their context as anything can come in this world" (JJT 15 p. 443) [V].

### ASL as a language

- **Not English.** "ASL is not simply English rendered in manual form." (ASL II) [V]
  - Sign language is not "simply a visual representation of oral speech (which it is not, of course)" (JJT 15 n. 37) [V].
  - ASL shows "some Pidgin Signed English structures" and initialised signs (ASL I) [V].
- **Grammar.** "As a visual language, ASL is actually not as inflected as the English or Latin languages." (ASL II) [V] It has "flexible word order, inflected classifiers, and preference for placing verbs at the ends of phrases" [V].
  - "The perceived absence of pronouns from ASL, however, is greatly exaggerated." (ASL I, on gendered pronouns) [V]
- **Lexicon.** "The theological deficiencies of ASL vocabulary are real" (ASL I) [V]. They can be filled, as Latin waited for *transubstantiatio* [V].
- **Language, not speech.** "what is necessary for sophisticated human communication is not speech, but language" (JJT 15 p. 433) [V]. He quotes P. Ogden: "Deafness is not about hearing but about communication." [V]
- **Writing.** "an accepted system for writing sign language does not yet exist" (Blog 2008) [V].
- **Responses.** "captioning is one-directional; people might read captioning, but they don't respond in it." "Sign languages do." (Blog 2008) [V]
- **Liturgical language.**
  - "A liturgical language is any language in which it is recognized by competent ecclesiastical authority" "that the minister can celebrate the rite licitly" (ASL II) [V].
  - Every translation answers to the Latin: "there is only one editio typica of the Mass, and it's in Latin" (ASL I) [V]. "it alone is the standard to which all translations are held" (ASL II) [V].
  - That English is liturgical means "the priest can confect the Eucharist directly in English without, say, having to whisper Latin words of consecration" (ASL II) [V].
- **Concepts in Latin.** It is wrong to imply "that concepts expressed in Latin cannot be rendered into sign (which they unquestionably can)" (JJT 15 n. 37) [V].
- **History.** After Lane, l'Epée offered Mass in French sign language "not later than May 1777 in the Church of St. Roch in Paris" [S]. Peters's claim: "these signed Masses could have been the first Masses celebrated in a vernacular language in the West" (JJT 15 n. 36) [V].
- **Community.** ASL is "the standard language of most deaf community Americans", though many deaf Americans "are not culturally Deaf" (Comments, 3 Jan 2008) [V].

### The 1965 letter, in his three readings

Observation: Peters reads its scope three ways.
- 1997: officials "have approved of celebrants speaking and signing simultaneously their parts" "of the liturgy, with the people responding in sign" (ASL I) [V].
- 2001: "the Holy See approved the use of sign language through the entire Mass by both priest and people" (ASL II) [V].
- 2008: the decree permits, "(in one paragraph, at least)", sign "along with speech" "in those parts of the Mass celebrated in the vernacular" (JJT 15 n. 37) [V]. The body has the Pope permitting sign "at Mass by priest and people alike" (p. 437) [V].

His transcription (NCOD page) holds both scopes [V]:
- The question asked whether sign "could be used in all those parts of the Mass" "that were in the vernacular".
- Its (a) asked "whether texts proffered by the celebrant could be at the same time spoken and signified with his hands".
- The Pope "has given his full approval to these suggestions".
- He added that "sign language could be used with and by deaf people through the Liturgy, whenever it was judged to be pastorally desirable".

## What this changes for SG1–SG8

All proposals.

- **SG1.** No change. Peters gives no notation or analysis of pronoun number. His remark on pronouns concerns gender.
- **SG2.** Scope each `form_reading` claim to the encodings its claimant names.
  - Peters's `r_lingual` rests on a sign language being "an immediate presentation" of concepts, "in contrast to writing". He enters it for languages he calls "independent authentic human languages", and ASL is "not simply English rendered in manual form".
  - He does not say whether it reaches a `code_of` encoding, which represents English as writing does.
  - So add to SG2: on a SEE-II form, `r_lingual` runs only where a claimant for that encoding is entered. Otherwise the case reports `info(no_claimant(r_lingual, en_see2))`.
- **SG3.** Name Regatillo, as Peters reads him, as a claimant for the lexicon-relative sense: words that "propose to the congregants such a sense by common understanding". This supports the everyday-lexicon branch as a reading of the hearing's lexicon, not a fault of the form. The outputs are unchanged.
- **SG4.** No change. Peters's "an accepted system for writing sign language does not yet exist" (2008) is a claimant for treating a notation as a claim, never as the encoding.
- **SG5.**
  - (a) Peters becomes a [V] claimant for `r_lingual` at SC 42 pp. 336 and 343–345, and McNamara stays a second.
  - (b) Enter the `r_vocal` claimants with their loci as Peters reports them: Cappello, Regatillo, Halligan, Hervé, and Noldin–Schmitt–Heinzel.
  - (c) The 1957 response is doctrinal, not governance, so it is not caught by `teaching_from_law`. Its bearing turns on the reading of *pronuntiat*:
    - `r_vocal`: spoken.
    - `r_lingual`: expressed (Peters); against the merely mental (McNamara).
    - Proposal: add a consecration variant. `q(valid_form(eucharist, ase_institution))` takes a value from the 1957 response, at its tier, on `r_vocal` only, and keeps both branches otherwise. The two dates enter as date claims.
    - For baptism SG5 stands, since no source addresses it beyond c. 849's "verborum forma".
  - (d) The 1965 letter carries two scope readings, vernacular parts only and the whole liturgy, each with its claimant (Peters 1997 and 2008; Peters 2001; the letter's own "through the Liturgy"). As practice each weighs.
  - (e) A ready test input: SC 42 n. 5 sets "Pope Paul VI's express approval" against a challenge to validity. Entered that way it must be refused (`teaching_from_law`).
  - (f) Practice enters as a claim with its claimant (Peters: "the modern praxis Ecclesiae"; "Ecclesiae praxis aliquando docet doctrinam Ecclesiae"). It has no tier and never fixes. A `valid_form` value resting on it alone is refused, as `mode_fixed_by_claim` is.
  - (g) Narrow the CAVEAT "The practice it leans on … has the celebrant voice the words" to approved practice. Peters reports unvoiced practice: deaf clergy "without vocalization over the past 40 years", and hearing priests who "frequently do not 'voice' the form of absolution" [S].
- **SG6.** No change to the outputs. Peters enters as a claimant for validity only ("Answer, Yes."), never for `r_verba` or `r_signa`. His site has no reading of c. 1104 §2. Reading SC 42 as favouring `r_verba` would be this record's inference, not his claim.
  - Variant B is unchanged. ASL II supports entering fidelity as a fact about the interpreter, "not the nature of sign language".
  - Optional variant A2: the assistant is a deaf priest who signs (Peters: deaf clergy "witnessed scores of weddings").
  - For an assistant outside ASL with no interpreter, "receives" (c. 1108 §2) stays an entered fact, never derived (rule 3). Report `info(assistant_outside_encoding)`, never a refusal: no text read decides it.
- **SG7.** Split the penitent's act from the form.
  - The signed confession carries no dispute: the `r_vocal` side concedes it (Cappello, "It does not go to the validity of the sacrament that confession be vocal").
  - The signed absolution carries it: `r_vocal` Cappello n. 68 and Halligan, against `r_lingual` Peters (J 73 p. 526).
  - In the interpreter variant, the interpreter is permitted, never required (c. 990 "Nemo prohibetur"; Propaganda Fide 1630; McManus). A case that enters an obligation to confess through an interpreter is refused.
  - Video confession (J 73) stays out: presence through a screen belongs to the senses step.
- **SG8.** No change. Nothing on Peters's site touches iconicity.
